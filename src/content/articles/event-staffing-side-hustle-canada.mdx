Event staffing in Canada is short-shift work at weddings, festivals, concerts, conferences, and corporate parties: serving food and drinks, bartending, setting up and tearing down, running coat check or registration, and helping guests. It costs little to start beyond black clothing, comfortable shoes, and any alcohol-service certificate your province requires, and it suits energetic, presentable people who like busy rooms and can stand for long shifts.

Event staffing is often booked through agencies, caterers, and venues rather than directly by clients. If you'd rather choose short tasks through an app, compare [odd-job apps](/side-hustles/odd-jobs-apps-side-hustle-canada/). Holiday-season options are in [seasonal holiday jobs in Canada](/side-hustles/seasonal-holiday-jobs-canada/) and [Halloween side hustles](/side-hustles/halloween-side-hustles-canada/).

## Who does event staffing in Canada suit?

Event staffing in Canada suits people who are punctual, friendly under pressure, and comfortable on their feet for six or more hours. It is a poor fit if you need weekday daytime hours only, if you dislike crowds or loud venues, or if you can't commit to arriving early for setup.

The calendar is uneven. Weddings and festivals cluster in warmer months, corporate parties cluster in November and December, and conferences run through the year in larger cities. Many people combine event staffing with another side hustle to smooth out the quiet weeks.

## Do you need Smart Serve or Serving It Right for event staffing?

You need provincially approved alcohol-service training if your event role involves selling or serving alcohol in Ontario or B.C., and other provinces have their own programs. Roles that don't touch alcohol, such as coat check or setup, generally don't need it, but many employers prefer staff who have it.

In Ontario, the [AGCO](https://www.agco.ca/en/alcohol/responsible-liquor-sale-service-and-delivery-training) says any person involved in the sale, service, sampling, or delivery of liquor, or in taking orders for liquor, must hold valid certification from an AGCO Board-approved training course. Smart Serve is approved for all liquor licensees and their employees, is listed at $44.95, and certifications must be renewed every five years.

In B.C., [Responsible Service BC](https://www.responsibleservicebc.gov.bc.ca/who-needs-serving-it-right) says Serving It Right certification is an operating requirement for licensed establishments, and that where a licensee has a catering endorsement, all licensees, managers, and servers working at events must complete the program. For other provinces, check the provincial liquor regulator's website before your first bar shift.

## Are event staff employees or self-employed in Canada?

Whether event staff are employees or self-employed in Canada depends on the actual working relationship, not the label on the job post. The CRA's page on [determining employment status](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/canada-pension-plan-cpp-employment-insurance-ei-rulings/employee-self-employed/determine-employment-status.html) says the factors differ depending on where the contract was formed: a common-law approach outside Quebec and the Civil Code of Québec inside Quebec. If you or the payer are unsure, you can ask the CRA for a CPP/EI ruling.

In practice, many staffing agencies and caterers put event staff on payroll and issue a T4. Some apps and small clients treat workers as independent contractors. Read the agreement, ask how you'll be paid, and keep records either way.

## How much can event staffing earn in Canada?

Event staffing earnings in Canada depend on the role, the employer, the province's minimum wage if you are an employee, and tips, so this guide does not publish a national rate. What you keep depends on:

- Hourly pay or flat shift rates, and whether tips or gratuities are shared
- Shift length, including setup and teardown time
- Alcohol-service roles, which may pay differently from setup roles
- Travel time and late-night transport costs
- Whether you're on payroll (taxes deducted) or self-employed (set aside yourself)

### Illustrative example

This is a teaching sketch with assumed numbers, not a quote or a market rate. Suppose a worker spends $45 on alcohol-service training and $70 on shoes: $45 + $70 = $115. Suppose a wedding shift is 7 hours at an assumed $18 an hour: 7 × $18 = $126 before deductions or a tax set-aside. One shift roughly covers the start-up spend; a second shift is the first that adds to your budget. Late transport and unpaid travel time reduce the real hourly figure.

## How do you start event staffing in Canada?

Starting event staffing in Canada means getting the right certificate, applying to agencies and caterers, and showing up early for your first shifts. The steps below follow that order.

### Step 1: Decide which roles you want

Setup, coat check, food running, serving, or bartending. Alcohol roles need the provincial certificate; setup roles usually don't.

### Step 2: Get alcohol-service training if needed

In Ontario, Smart Serve through an [AGCO-approved program](https://www.agco.ca/en/alcohol/responsible-liquor-sale-service-and-delivery-training). In B.C., [Serving It Right](https://www.responsibleservicebc.gov.bc.ca/who-needs-serving-it-right). Elsewhere, check the provincial liquor regulator.

### Step 3: Assemble your event outfit

All-black clothing and non-slip shoes cover most dress codes. Ask about any uniform the employer provides.

### Step 4: Apply to agencies, caterers, and venues

Event staffing agencies, catering companies, hotels, and wedding venues all hire short-shift staff. Apply before the busy season starts.

### Step 5: Clarify pay and status

Ask whether you'll be paid as an employee or contractor, how tips work, and when you'll be paid. Keep a copy of any agreement.

### Step 6: Work your first shift like a trial

Arrive early, ask who your lead is, learn the floor plan, and stay until teardown is done. Employers rebook people who make the night easier.

### Step 7: Track every shift

Record dates, hours, pay, tips, and travel. If you're self-employed, set aside tax from each payment.

## What mistakes are common in event staffing in Canada?

Common event staffing mistakes in Canada are serving alcohol without the required certificate, assuming your tax status, and wearing the wrong shoes. Each one is easy to avoid.

- **No alcohol certificate.** Serving without required training puts you and the licensee at risk.
- **Assuming you're self-employed.** Many staffing roles are employment; your tax filing depends on which.
- **Uncomfortable shoes.** A long shift on hard floors in the wrong shoes is miserable.
- **Missing setup time.** Arriving at the guest start time instead of the call time is a fast way to lose rebookings.
- **No late-night travel plan.** Events end late; plan the ride home.

## Which tax rules apply to event staffing in Canada?

Event staffing income in Canada is taxed according to your status. If you're an employee, your employer deducts tax, CPP, and EI and issues a T4. If you're genuinely self-employed, you report income on [Form T2125, Statement of Business or Professional Activities](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html) and pay both halves of the [Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html) contribution on net self-employment income. The CRA explains how status is decided on its page on [determining employment status](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/canada-pension-plan-cpp-employment-insurance-ei-rulings/employee-self-employed/determine-employment-status.html).

This is not tax, legal, or insurance advice.

Self-employed event workers follow the [$30,000 small-supplier test](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html) for GST/HST across all side-hustle revenue. Employees don't register for GST/HST on their wages. Tips count too: for employees, the CRA's [line 10400 page](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/personal-income/line-10400-other-employment-income.html) says to report tips and gratuities that are not on your T4 slip as other employment income; self-employed workers include them in business income. See [how to report side hustle income to the CRA](/side-hustles/report-side-hustle-income-cra-canada/) and [how much tax to set aside](/side-hustles/how-much-tax-set-aside-side-hustle-canada/).
