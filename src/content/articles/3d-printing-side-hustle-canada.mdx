## Who does a 3D printing side hustle in Canada suit?

A 3D printing side hustle in Canada suits someone who will design a narrow object, throw away bad prints, and talk to a buyer about size and colour. It suits a spare room or a garage you can ventilate. It does not suit a closed bedroom, a baby in the next room, or a plan that depends on printing other people's characters.

Original niche designs beat popular files. A bracket a local shop needs, a replacement knob you measured, or a small product you designed is work a stranger's famous figurine is not. The famous file is also the one a rights holder can challenge.

## Which designs can you sell from a 3D printer in Canada?

Sell designs you created, or designs whose licence explicitly allows you to sell printed objects. A download button is not that licence.

[CIPO](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/learn-copyright-canadian-intellectual-property-office) describes copyright as the sole right to produce, reproduce, publish, or perform a work, or a substantial part of it. Section 3 of the [Copyright Act](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-42/section-3.html) states that sole right, including reproduction in any material form. Printing a model from a file is making a new object from the work. Read the file's licence. "Personal use," "non-commercial," and a Creative Commons licence that reserves commercial rights are different sentences. If the licence does not say you may sell prints, assume you may not.

Trademarks are a second gate. CIPO says a trademark can be words, symbols, or a three-dimensional shape used to distinguish goods. Section 19 of the [Trademarks Act](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/T-13/section-19.html) gives the owner of a registered trademark the exclusive right to use it throughout Canada for the registered goods or services. Section 20 deems that right infringed when a person who is not entitled to use it sells or advertises goods in association with a confusing trademark. A team logo, a character name, or a brand on a print is that problem. Start with CIPO's [trademark overview](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/learn-trademarks-canadian-intellectual-property-office) before you put someone else's mark on an object you sell. This is not legal advice.

## How do you print without filling the room with fumes?

Print in Canada with the filament's safety data sheet open, the printer enclosed or exhausted, and people sleeping somewhere else. The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety fact sheet on [additive manufacturing](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/additive_manufacturing.pdf) says heating 3D-printing materials can expose users to hazardous aerosols, volatile organic compounds, and ultrafine particles. It gives PLA as an example that emits VOCs, including formaldehyde, and respirable particulates, and ABS as an example that emits ultrafine particles and VOCs. It says short- and long-term health effects are not yet well understood, so exposure should stay as low as reasonably achievable.

The same sheet's controls include a partial or full enclosure, local exhaust at the printer, more room ventilation, limiting higher-emitting polymers, and following the manufacturer's instructions and the safety data sheet. It also flags heat, moving parts, electricity, and ultraviolet light on resin machines. A desktop printer in an apartment still deserves those controls. Crack a window, run the exhaust the machine has, and leave the room while it prints. Do not sleep next to it. Do not sand or paint in the same closed room without reading the coating's label.

Resin printing adds liquid chemicals and UV. CCOHS discusses vat polymerization and tells workplaces to use the safety data sheet. If you are not set up to handle resin, gloves, and wash solvent, stay with filament until you are.

## How do you start selling 3D prints in Canada?

Start by printing one useful object you designed, showing it to a real buyer, and only then buying more filament.

### Step 1: Pick a narrow object you can design

Measure a problem you have seen: a shop's missing clip, a hobby part, a simple household piece with no logo on it. Draw it yourself or pay someone for a design you are allowed to sell. The drawing is the product. The printer is the tool.

### Step 2: Print samples and count the failures

The first prints will fail. That filament is a cost, covered in the [startup cost guide](/side-hustles/3d-printing-startup-costs-canada/). A <AffiliateLink program="amazon-ca" label="spool of filament" search="PLA filament 1.75 mm" /> is the supply you will actually use up. One spool is enough to learn. A second printer can wait. Match the plastic to what the safety sheet and the machine manual allow.

### Step 3: Set up ventilation before a long job

Enclosure, exhaust, and a room you can leave. Read the CCOHS sheet and the filament SDS before you run an eight-hour print overnight in a living space.

### Step 4: Find one buyer who is not a marketplace search

A local business, a club, or a neighbour who needs ten of something is a better first order than a public listing of a generic figure. Repeat buyers are the point. Engraved gifts for the same kind of local customer are a cousin hustle: [laser engraving in Canada](/side-hustles/laser-engraving-side-hustle-canada/).

### Step 5: List only after the licence and the handmade rules are clear

If you use a marketplace, read its current rules on what counts as handmade or original. The [Etsy shop guide](/side-hustles/etsy-shop-side-hustle-canada/) is the fee and setup overview. It is not a promise that every print qualifies. Photograph the object you printed, including layer lines if a buyer would feel them.

### Step 6: Price your time after the printer stops

Support removal, a light sand of a surface the SDS allows, a photo, a mailer, and a post office trip are the hours. The printer running unattended is not those hours. Failed prints still consumed filament.

### Step 7: Reorder filament for designs that sold

Buy the colour that sold. Keep a note of grams used, including failures, so the next quote is closer.

## What mistakes are common with 3D printing for profit in Canada?

Common mistakes are selling someone else's character, printing in a bedroom, and quoting a price that ignores failed prints and packing.

- **A trending file.** If you did not design it and the licence does not allow sales, do not list it.
- **A logo "because the customer asked."** A customer's request does not grant a trademark.
- **No exhaust.** CCOHS names the emissions. An enclosure with nowhere for the air to go is a box of the same air.
- **Forgetting fulfilment.** A print that takes six hours of machine time can take another half hour of your hands, plus a parcel.
- **Buying a second machine** before ten of the first design have sold.

## Which tax rules apply to a 3D printing side hustle in Canada?

Selling prints in Canada is generally business income on [Form T2125](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html). Self-employed people pay both portions of the [Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html) contribution on net self-employment income. Filament and mailers are costs to record. A printer that lasts for years may be capital cost allowance on the business-use portion. The cost guide links the CRA page for that.

GST/HST follows the [$30,000 small-supplier threshold](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html), not the [taxi and ride-sharing](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-specific-situations/taxi-ride-sharing-drivers.html) first-dollar rule.
