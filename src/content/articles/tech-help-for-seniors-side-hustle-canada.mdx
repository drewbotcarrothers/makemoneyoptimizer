## Who does tech help for seniors in Canada suit?

Tech help for seniors in Canada suits people who can explain a setting twice, wait while someone tries it, and leave without "fixing" things they were not asked to touch. Students, retirees, and anyone who has already helped a relative with a phone are the usual fit. It is a poor fit if you are impatient, if you want remote-only work, or if you would be tempted to upsell a device the person does not need.

The visit is in the home or at a table the client chooses. You are a guest. Some community centres and libraries ask outside presenters to register, and some want a police record check. There is no single national rule. Ask that centre what it requires before you put up a sign. Do not tell people you are "from the library" or "from the government."

Toronto Public Library's [Book a Librarian](https://tpl.ca/using-the-library/services/book-a-librarian/) page offers a free 30–60 minute appointment for help with research or library services, and it says customers can learn through that appointment. That is the library's service, staffed by the library. It is not a booth for a side hustle. If you mention it, mention it as the free option a person might try first. A paid visit is for help at home, on the device and the Wi-Fi they actually use. Do not solicit clients inside a library program unless that branch has invited you to.

## What should a tech help visit include in Canada?

A tech help visit in Canada should include one agreed problem, a price said before you start, and a short note the client can read after you leave. It should not include storing passwords, installing remote-control software they did not ask for, or advice about banking, benefits, or taxes.

| In a normal first visit | Leave it out |
| --- | --- |
| Join a home Wi-Fi network they named | A new internet plan you get a commission on, unless you say so and they still want it |
| Enlarge text, turn on captions, open email | A new email account that locks them out of the old one |
| Place one video-call test with a family member they choose | Access to their banking app |
| A written card: what you tapped, and how to undo it | Your copy of their passphrase |
| A next visit only if they ask | A subscription you started on their card |

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's [Secure your accounts](https://www.getcybersafe.gc.ca/en/secure-your-accounts) page says using unique and complex passphrases and passwords for every account and device is one of the best ways to keep personal information safe, and that password managers help people keep track of logins. If a client wants that, set it up on their device, in an account they control, and let them hold the master passphrase. Do not keep a notebook of client passwords "in case they forget." If they forget, they use the recovery method on their account, with you beside them, or they call the company themselves.

ISED's page on [getting consent to send email](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canada-anti-spam-legislation/en/getting-consent-send-email) says Canada's anti-spam legislation requires consent before commercial electronic messages such as emails or texts, and that some businesses also have duties under the [Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/) for personal information. A phone number an adult child gave you so you can confirm a Tuesday visit is not a licence to add that parent to a marketing list. Alberta, British Columbia, and Quebec have their own private-sector privacy laws. Read the Office of the Privacy Commissioner's "which privacy law applies" material before you build a contact list. ## How do you start tech help for seniors in Canada?

Starting tech help for seniors in Canada means deciding which problems you will take, telling people how you charge, and getting the first visits through people who already trust you.

### Step 1: Write a short menu

Examples: Wi-Fi, email on a phone, a video call, a printer, photo sorting, and text size. Write what you will not do: banking, taxes, password storage, and anything that needs a shop repair.

### Step 2: Set a visit length and a price you can say

A one-hour visit is easier to explain than "until it's fixed." There is no official Canadian rate for this help. Time your own practice sessions with a relative who agrees, and price from that. The sketch further down is not a market rate.

### Step 3: Practise the explanation

Sit with one device and narrate each tap. If you cannot explain it, you are not ready to charge for it.

### Step 4: Tell adult children and community programs

Adult children are often the person who books, because they are the one who gets the call when the tablet fails before an appointment. A seniors' centre, a faith community, or a residents' association may allow a short introduction if you ask the organizer. Do not put flyers on cars in the parking lot unless they said yes. Knocking rules, if you go door to door, are in [door-to-door marketing for side hustles in Canada](/side-hustles/door-to-door-marketing-side-hustle-canada/).

### Step 5: Carry almost nothing

A charged phone, a notebook, and maybe a spare cable. ### Step 6: Leave a paper note

Three lines: what you changed, how to undo it, and your phone number. Skip the email list. If they want a follow-up text, ask in person and keep the message about that visit.

### Step 7: Stop at the edge of advice

You can show someone how to open a government site. You cannot tell them how to file a return or whether a benefit letter is genuine. Point them to the agency's phone number on the letter. ## What mistakes are common in tech help for seniors in Canada?

Common mistakes in tech help for seniors in Canada are taking the device away and tapping in silence, saving passwords, and turning a visit into a sales pitch.

- **Doing it for them.** They need to try the last tap while you watch, or the problem returns the same evening.
- **A password notebook.** Get Cyber Safe's point is that the person can protect their own accounts. Your notebook is a second copy you can lose.
- **Remote access left running.** If you install a remote tool, remove it before you leave unless they clearly want it and know how to close it.
- **New hardware on the first visit.** A cable you already warned them about is different from a laptop they did not ask to buy.
- **Gift-card payment.** If anyone, including you, asks an older adult to pay a helper in gift cards, stop. That pattern is a scam signal. Read [side hustle scams](/side-hustles/side-hustle-scams-canada/).
- **Winter-only panic.** After the holidays, new phones and tablets show up in households. That is a real season for this work, and it is still one visit at a time.
