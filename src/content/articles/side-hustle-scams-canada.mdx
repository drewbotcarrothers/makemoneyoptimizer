<QuickAnswer>

- **The pattern:** a side hustle offer that asks you to pay, forward money, or deposit a cheque before you can "unlock" earnings is the pattern the [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) describes as job fraud.
- **2025 scale:** job fraud was 2,148 reports, 1,726 victims, and $50.6 million in reported losses on the CAFC's [top 10 frauds in 2025](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2026/02/top-fraud-2025-fraudes-plus-courantes-eng.htm) page.
- **What to do:** do not pay a fee to get a job, do not forward money, and report it to local police and the [Report Cybercrime and Fraud](https://reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca/) line at 1-888-495-8501.
- **Deeper reads:** [task scams](#how-do-task-scams-work-in-canada) and [fake cheque and overpayment scams](#how-do-fake-cheque-and-overpayment-scams-work-in-canada).

</QuickAnswer>

Side hustle scams in Canada are job, task, and marketplace frauds that copy the shape of real extra-income work: a text about easy tasks, a cheque for a "car wrap," or a buyer who overpays a Kijiji listing. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre's [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) says many frauds target people looking for a job, and its warning signs include unlock payments, a balance you cannot withdraw, and requests to receive or forward money.

This is general information, not legal advice. A real mystery-shop assignment is different from the scam version; see [mystery shopping](/side-hustles/mystery-shopping-side-hustle-canada/).

## How common are side hustle and job scams in Canada?

Job fraud is one of the larger fraud types Canadians report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, and the totals count only what was reported. The figures below are from the CAFC's [top 10 frauds in 2025](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2026/02/top-fraud-2025-fraudes-plus-courantes-eng.htm) page.

The CAFC's [2022 annual report](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/annual-reports-2022-rapports-annuels-eng.htm) says only 5 to 10 percent of fraud connected to Canadian victims is reported to the centre. This guide uses the 2025 counts as published, without multiplying them.

| 2025 CAFC figure | What the top-10 page states |
| --- | --- |
| All fraud reports | Over 112,000 reports; more than $704 million in reported losses |
| Job fraud | 2,148 reports; 1,726 victims; $50.6 million |
| Merchandise fraud | 2,596 reports; 2,222 victims; $11.7 million on the frequency table |
| Under-reporting caveat | 5 to 10 percent, stated in the 2022 annual report, not as a 2025 multiplier |

## What are 12 side hustle scam red flags in Canada?

Twelve red flags in Canada come from the CAFC's job, merchandise, and payment-method pages. Any one of them is a reason to stop. Several together are the usual script.

1. **You must pay to start, upgrade, or unlock earnings.** The [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) lists upfront deposits, "upgrade fees," and "unlock payments" as warning signs. The detail is in the [task scam guide](#how-do-task-scams-work-in-canada).
2. **The app shows a balance you cannot withdraw.** The same page lists this as a sign.
3. **A small first payout, then a demand for a deposit.** This is the web-survey and fake online-store variation.
4. **A cheque arrives before any work, and you are told to send part of it on.** The counterfeit-cheque and car-wrap variations work this way. The payment-method [bulletin](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/03/bulletin-3-eng.htm) says that if a cheque returns, you are responsible to repay the full amount.
5. **A buyer overpays and asks for the difference back.** The CAFC [merchandise page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/merchandise-marchandises-eng.htm) calls this overpayment fraud. The original payment is later fraudulent.
6. **A fake e-transfer or payment email asks you to "accept" funds.** The merchandise page says the message can be built to steal banking credentials.
7. **You are told to buy gift cards and send the numbers.** The mystery-shopper variation on the job page uses gift cards "to confirm they work," plus a survey that the CAFC calls a prop.
8. **The offer arrived from webmail, for a job you did not apply for, using a real company's name.** The May 30, 2024 alert on the job page says fraudsters use the names of real Canadian companies for crypto "boosting" tasks.
9. **You must install their software or app to do simple tasks.** This is how the boosting variation starts.
10. **You are asked to recruit other people to earn more.** The job page says this is pyramid selling and that promoting a pyramid-selling scheme is a criminal offence in Canada.
11. **The "job" is to receive money and forward it.** The financial-agent variation calls this being a money mule. The page says you could be arrested for money laundering. The payment bulletin says the same about e-transfers you are told to pass on.
12. **Someone later offers to recover the loss, or claims to be the Anti-Fraud Centre and asks for money.** The 2025 top-10 page lists recovery-pitch fraud at $25.9 million in reported losses. The centre's own [home page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/index-eng.htm) says fraudsters impersonate the CAFC and that the centre does not contact individuals to request money.

### Illustrative example

This sketch shows the money flow the CAFC describes. It is not a typical loss, a case file, or a figure from the 2025 table.

**Illustrative example.** Suppose a message offers car-wrap work and a cheque arrives. The CAFC's car-wrap variation says fraudsters pitch earnings of $300 to $500 per week, then send a cheque and tell you to deposit it, withdraw some of the funds, and send money to a "graphics company." Suppose, as an assumption only, the cheque is $2,400 and you are told to forward $1,900. If the cheque is counterfeit and returns, the payment bulletin says you are responsible to repay the full amount. In the sketch, the $1,900 has left your account and the $2,400 deposit does not stay. The $300 to $500 pitch is the fraudster's claim on the CAFC page, not a wage.

## How do legitimate side hustles in Canada differ from these offers?

Legitimate side hustles in Canada pay you for work you have done. They do not ask you to fund the payout. A marketplace sale is paid by the buyer at the price you agreed, in person when you can, which is the CAFC's advice on its [merchandise habits page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2025/10/avoid-merchandise-evitez-eng.htm). A mystery-shop company pays after the report. An odd-job app pays for a completed task without an "unlock" deposit.

| What you see | Scam pattern on the CAFC pages | Ordinary side hustle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who contacts whom | Unsolicited text, WhatsApp, or email | You apply on the company's own site |
| Money direction | You pay, forward, or refund | They pay you after the work |
| Cheque or gift card | Deposit first, or buy cards and send codes | Not how you get hired |
| Balance on a screen | Visible, not withdrawable | A payout to your account |

[Odd-job apps](/side-hustles/odd-jobs-apps-side-hustle-canada/) and [mystery shopping](/side-hustles/mystery-shopping-side-hustle-canada/) are the legitimate cousins of the scripts above. Read the scam section of the mystery-shopping guide before you send ID or a void cheque.

## How do task scams work in Canada?

A task scam in Canada is a fake job that pays, or pretends to pay, for tapping through "orders," product boosts, or surveys, then tells you to deposit your own money to unlock higher commissions or to withdraw the balance. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre's [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) describes that sequence for crypto boosting, web surveys, and fake online-store work. A payment you must buy back is not a wage. The CAFC lists unlock payments, upgrade fees, and upfront deposits as warning signs of job fraud, and its advice is never to send deposits or pay fees for a job. If the only way to reach the balance is another deposit, the balance is the prop.

### How do boosting, survey, and fake-store task scams work in Canada?

The CAFC job page describes three task variations. In crypto boosting, which its May 30, 2024 alert says uses the names of real Canadian companies, you install the fraudsters' software, get "orders," may receive a small commission, and are then told to pay fees to "move up a level," with funds stuck in a crypto account you cannot withdraw. Fake online-store and web-survey jobs arrive through social ads and messages on WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram, Messenger, or TikTok, then demand deposits to upgrade the account, fix an "error," or continue.

The centre's [payment-methods bulletin](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/03/bulletin-3-eng.htm) says fraudsters commonly request cryptocurrency for job fraud because it is harder to trace and very unlikely to be reversed.

| CAFC variation | What they ask you to do | What the page says happens next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crypto boosting | Install their software and complete tasks | A small commission, then fees to level up; crypto you cannot withdraw |
| Fake online store | Process orders for a commission | Deposits to upgrade, fix an "error," or unlock commissions |
| Web surveys | Finish a few paid surveys | A problem on the account, then a deposit to continue |
| Recruiting | Bring in other people for higher pay | The page calls this pyramid selling, a criminal offence to promote |

#### Illustrative example

**Illustrative example.** This is the shape of the survey variation, with assumed amounts. It is not a CAFC case and not a typical loss. Suppose the app pays a supposed $8 commission after three short surveys, which you can see but not withdraw. It then says a $60 deposit will "unlock" the $8 and open a higher level. The job page's description of this variation is that the balance is fake and the deposits are the fraud. Sending the $60 does not turn the $8 into wages. It is $60 you should not send. Verify any company on its own website and by calling a phone number you looked up, not the number in the message.

## How do fake cheque and overpayment scams work in Canada?

Fake cheque and overpayment scams in Canada trick side hustlers into sending real money against a payment that does not stick. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre's [payment-methods bulletin](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/03/bulletin-3-eng.htm) says suspects send fraudulent cheques, ask victims to deposit them, and tell them to transfer the funds before the cheque has cleared. If the cheque returns, you repay the full amount. A marketplace version, on the [merchandise page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/merchandise-marchandises-eng.htm), is the buyer who "overpays" and wants the difference wired back.

### How do fake cheque scams target side hustlers in Canada?

The CAFC [job page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) describes three cheque-first versions:

- **Counterfeit cheque jobs.** A text or email answers a resume posted online, for roles such as caregiver, administrative assistant, or data entry clerk. You are asked to cash a cheque and return some of the funds.

- **Mystery shopper cheques.** A notice says you were selected as a mystery shopper and includes a cheque to deposit and partly transfer on. You may also be told to buy gift cards and send the numbers "to confirm they work," with surveys the CAFC calls a prop.

- **Car wrapping.** An unsolicited message pitches $300 to $500 per week for wrapping a vehicle, then sends a cheque; you deposit it and send money to a "graphics company." The weekly figures are the fraudster's pitch on the CAFC page, not a rate this site quotes as pay.

Money that your bank lets you withdraw is not proof the cheque is good.

### How do overpayment and fake e-transfer scams target sellers in Canada?

The CAFC [merchandise page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/merchandise-marchandises-eng.htm) says fraudsters contact people selling on social media or classifieds, often claiming to be out of town and buying unseen. In the overpayment tactic, you receive more than the asking price, are told to wire the excess back immediately, and later find the payment was fraudulent (compromised accounts, fake cheques, or stolen cards).

In the e-transfer tactic, an official-looking "etransfer" email or text asks you to accept a payment so it can steal banking credentials. The CAFC note [protecting yourself online](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/10/protecting-yourself-protegez-vous-eng.htm) adds that the message can be a request for funds, and that sellers are asked to ship and send tracking before a fake "pending" payment is released. It says not to transfer funds, for example to a shipping company, to receive payment for an item you are selling.

### What should Canadian sellers and gig workers do instead?

Keep the money moving in one direction: the other person pays you, and you do not pay them to be paid. The CAFC's October 2025 [merchandise habits page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2025/10/avoid-merchandise-evitez-eng.htm) says to beware of overpayments and overseas buyers who will not see the item, and to pick up and pay in person when you can. Do not wire an "extra" amount, deposit a stranger's cheque, or click a buyer's e-transfer link or QR code; the online-protection note says QR codes sent to sellers "so you can receive payment" have been used to collect online-banking details. As a buyer, pay in a way you can dispute.

#### Illustrative example

**Illustrative example.** Suppose you listed a dresser for $80, an assumed price, not a market quote. A buyer sends a payment of $300 and asks you to forward $220 for a courier. That shape is the overpayment tactic on the merchandise page: more than the asking price, then an immediate transfer of the excess. If the $300 later reverses, the sketch leaves you short the $220 you sent and without a trustworthy payment for the dresser. Do not ship the dresser against that message. The $80 and $300 are assumptions so the arithmetic is visible. They are not CAFC statistics.

### What do you do if you already deposited the cheque in Canada?

If you already deposited the cheque, contact your bank, stop sending funds, and report it using the steps in the next section. Do not send a second payment because the first one is "stuck," and do not give account access to someone offering to fix the cheque. This page cannot tell you what your bank will do on a particular deposit. Ask the bank. A fraudulent payment that reverses is not income you keep; how genuine personal sales are taxed is in [is selling on Kijiji or Facebook Marketplace taxable](/side-hustles/is-selling-on-kijiji-taxable-canada/).
## How do you report a side hustle scam in Canada?

Report a side hustle scam in Canada to your local police and to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, and tell the bank if money already left the account. Stop contact first. The Competition Bureau's page on [how to report fraud and scams](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/fraud-and-scams/tips-and-advice/how-report-fraud-and-scams-canada) lists the steps below, and says the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre estimates that less than 5% of fraud victims report to law enforcement.

### Where do you report a side hustle scam in Canada?

Use both doors. The police report is the local record; the centre collects reports from across the country. The Bureau's page says to call the police especially if you lost money. If nobody is in immediate danger, use the non-emergency number or online form on your police service's own website; there is no single national non-emergency number. Ask for a file number and keep it for the bank and the centre. If someone is in danger or you need urgent help, call 911.

| Step | Who | What they asked for on the official page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | You | Names, phone numbers, web addresses, screenshots, receipts |
| 2 | Local police | A report, and the file number they give you |
| 3 | Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre | [reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca](https://reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca/) or 1-888-495-8501 |
| 4 | Bank, card, or payment app | Notice that the transfer was fraud, via the number on the card |
| 5 | The website or app | The site's own report-abuse or report-an-ad link |
| 6 | Competition Bureau, if the problem is a misleading ad | Complaint form or 1-800-348-5358 |

The centre is jointly managed by the RCMP, the Competition Bureau, and the Ontario Provincial Police, according to the Bureau's [February 2025 notice](https://www.canada.ca/en/competition-bureau/news/2025/02/fraud-prevention-month-to-focus-on-impersonation-fraud-one-of-the-fastest-growing-forms-of-fraud.html).

### What should you write down before you call?

Write down the facts while the messages are still in the phone. The Competition Bureau's reporting page asks you to keep the name used, what they wanted, when it happened, the phone number, the exact web address, and copies of emails, texts, receipts, and cancelled cheques. Add screenshots of the sales page, checkout, and payment confirmation.

Keep those files in one folder; the same page says they may be evidence.

### How do you report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre?

Report to the centre online at [reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca](https://reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca/) or by phone at 1-888-495-8501, the number on both the job-scam page and the Bureau's page. File even if you only received the message and did not pay; the Bureau's 2025 notice says a report with no financial loss still goes to the centre. The centre does not take payment to investigate and does not call you to ask for a fee. Hang up on anyone who says they are the centre and then asks for money, gift cards, or remote access.

### When should you call the bank, the platform, and the Competition Bureau?

Call the financial institution as soon as money has moved. The Bureau's step 4 says to notify the bank, credit union, card issuer, or payment service that moved the money and tell them you are reporting fraud. Use the number on your card, statement, or official app, never one inside the scam message.

If the offer lived on a website, a marketplace, or a social network, use that site's report link. The Bureau names Facebook, eBay, Kijiji, and dating sites as examples, and says the link is often called "report abuse" or "report an ad." An app with its own help form gets the same treatment: report the profile inside the app, and keep the confirmation.

Misleading advertising is a Competition Bureau matter even when it is not a theft. The reporting page says you can report false or misleading marketing with the Bureau's online complaint form or by telephone at 1-800-348-5358. A course page that invents earnings is that kind of report. The buying-side warning signs are in [how to spot a course funnel](/side-hustles/course-funnel-red-flags-canada/).

If the scam used your identity, the same Bureau page says to contact both national credit bureaus and ask for a fraud alert: Equifax at 1-888-836-6351 and TransUnion at 1-800-663-9980. If you think your social insurance number was stolen, it says to contact Service Canada at 1-866-274-2267. Investment scams can also go to your provincial securities regulator through the Canadian Securities Administrators, which the Bureau lists on that page.

### What should you stop doing after you report?

Stop paying: a second fee to release a refund is the same scam, and so is a stranger offering to recover the loss for a fee. Stop installing software the sender asks for. Tell anyone whose name or photo was used, if you can do that without sending them money. Money you lost is not side hustle income. A small real payment for real work, if there was any, is a separate question in [how to report side hustle income](/side-hustles/report-side-hustle-income-cra-canada/). This page does not tell you how to deduct a fraud loss.
