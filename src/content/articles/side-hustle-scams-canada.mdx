Side hustle scams in Canada are job, task, and marketplace frauds that copy the shape of real extra-income work: a text about easy tasks, a cheque for a "car wrap," or a buyer who overpays a Kijiji listing. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre's [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) says many frauds target people looking for a job, and its warning signs include unlock payments, a balance you cannot withdraw, and requests to receive or forward money.

This is general information, not legal advice. The two close reads are [why "pay to unlock your earnings" is a task scam](#how-do-task-scams-work-in-canada) and [fake cheque and overpayment scams](#how-do-fake-cheque-and-overpayment-scams-work-in-canada). A real mystery-shop assignment is a different thing; the scam version is in [mystery shopping](/side-hustles/mystery-shopping-side-hustle-canada/).

## How common are side hustle and job scams in Canada?

Job fraud is one of the larger fraud types Canadians report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, and the published totals count only what was reported. On the CAFC's [top 10 frauds in 2025](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2026/02/top-fraud-2025-fraudes-plus-courantes-eng.htm) page, the centre received over 112,000 fraud reports involving more than $704 million in reported losses. Job fraud appears on both the frequency list and the dollar-loss list: 2,148 reports, 1,726 victims, and $50.6 million. Merchandise fraud, which includes marketplace scams, was 2,596 reports, 2,222 victims, and $11.7 million on the frequency table.

The CAFC's [2022 annual report](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/annual-reports-2022-rapports-annuels-eng.htm) says its statistics are based on reports received, and that only 5 to 10 percent of fraud connected to Canadian victims is reported to the centre. That sentence is the centre's caveat about reporting, not a licence to multiply the 2025 dollar figures by ten. This guide uses the 2025 counts as published.

| 2025 CAFC figure | What the top-10 page states |
| --- | --- |
| All fraud reports | Over 112,000 reports; more than $704 million in reported losses |
| Job fraud | 2,148 reports; 1,726 victims; $50.6 million |
| Merchandise fraud | 2,596 reports; 2,222 victims; $11.7 million on the frequency table |
| Under-reporting caveat | 5 to 10 percent, stated in the 2022 annual report, not as a 2025 multiplier |

## What are 12 side hustle scam red flags in Canada?

Twelve red flags in Canada come from the CAFC's job, merchandise, and payment-method pages. Any one of them is a reason to stop. Several together are the usual script.

1. **You must pay to start, upgrade, or unlock earnings.** The [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) lists upfront deposits, "upgrade fees," and "unlock payments" as warning signs. The detail is in the [task scam guide](#how-do-task-scams-work-in-canada).
2. **The app shows a balance you cannot withdraw.** The same page says you may see funds and still be unable to withdraw what you deposited or "earned."
3. **A small first payout, then a demand for a deposit.** The web-survey and fake online-store variation says victims may receive a small initial payout, then be asked to deposit money to continue.
4. **A cheque arrives before any work, and you are told to send part of it on.** The counterfeit-cheque and car-wrap variations work this way. The payment-method [bulletin](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/03/bulletin-3-eng.htm) says that if a cheque returns, you are responsible to repay the full amount.
5. **A buyer overpays and asks for the difference back.** The CAFC [merchandise page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/merchandise-marchandises-eng.htm) calls this overpayment fraud. The original payment is later fraudulent.
6. **A fake e-transfer or payment email asks you to "accept" funds.** That page says the message can be built to steal banking credentials. A [later CAFC note](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/10/protecting-yourself-protegez-vous-eng.htm) also warns that the message can be a request for money, not a payment to you.
7. **You are told to buy gift cards and send the numbers.** The mystery-shopper variation on the job page uses gift cards "to confirm they work," plus a survey that the CAFC calls a prop.
8. **The offer arrived from webmail, for a job you did not apply for, using a real company's name.** The May 30, 2024 alert on the job page says fraudsters use the names of real Canadian companies for crypto "boosting" tasks.
9. **You must install their software or app to do simple tasks.** The boosting variation starts with software the fraudsters created, then "orders" or "tasks."
10. **You are asked to recruit other people to earn more.** The job page says this is pyramid selling and that promoting a pyramid-selling scheme is a criminal offence in Canada.
11. **The "job" is to receive money and forward it.** The financial-agent variation calls this being a money mule. The page says you could be arrested for money laundering. The payment bulletin says the same about e-transfers you are told to pass on.
12. **Someone later offers to recover the loss, or claims to be the Anti-Fraud Centre and asks for money.** The 2025 top-10 page lists recovery-pitch fraud at $25.9 million in reported losses. The centre's own [home page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/index-eng.htm) says fraudsters impersonate the CAFC and that the centre does not contact individuals to request money.

## How do legitimate side hustles in Canada differ from these offers?

Legitimate side hustles in Canada pay you for work you have done. They do not ask you to fund the payout. A marketplace sale is paid by the buyer at the price you agreed, in person when you can, which is the CAFC's advice on its [merchandise habits page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2025/10/avoid-merchandise-evitez-eng.htm). A mystery-shop company pays after the report. An odd-job app pays for a completed task without an "unlock" deposit.

| What you see | Scam pattern on the CAFC pages | Ordinary side hustle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who contacts whom | Unsolicited text, WhatsApp, or email | You apply on the company's own site |
| Money direction | You pay, forward, or refund | They pay you after the work |
| Cheque or gift card | Deposit first, or buy cards and send codes | Not how you get hired |
| Balance on a screen | Visible, not withdrawable | A payout to your account |

[Odd-job apps](/side-hustles/odd-jobs-apps-side-hustle-canada/) and [mystery shopping](/side-hustles/mystery-shopping-side-hustle-canada/) are the legitimate cousins of the scripts above. Read the scam section of the mystery-shopping guide before you send ID or a void cheque. Selling a used item is covered in [is selling on Kijiji taxable](/side-hustles/is-selling-on-kijiji-taxable-canada/), which is a tax question, not a reason to ship goods against a fake payment.

## What should you do if you already sent money in Canada?

If you already sent money in Canada, stop contact, tell your bank or card issuer, and report the fraud. The CAFC job page says anyone who suspects they have been targeted should report it to local police and to the Report Cybercrime and Fraud website, or by phone at 1-888-495-8501. The payment bulletin adds that if you were not a victim, you can still report the attempt to the centre.

Do not send a further "fee" to unlock a refund. Do not give remote access to someone who says they are recovering the funds. The centre's impersonation alert says the CAFC does not contact people to request money.

A small real payment you received before the scam turned is not a reason to keep depositing. If you were paid for actual work and you are unsure what belongs on a tax return, that is a separate question in [how to report side hustle income](/side-hustles/report-side-hustle-income-cra-canada/). Money you lost to a fraud is not income. This page does not tell you how to claim a loss.

## How do task scams work in Canada?

A task scam in Canada is a fake job that pays, or pretends to pay, for tapping through "orders," product boosts, or surveys, then tells you to deposit your own money to unlock higher commissions or to withdraw the balance. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre's [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) describes that sequence for crypto boosting, web surveys, and fake online-store work. A payment you must buy back is not a wage.

### Why is "pay to unlock your earnings" a task scam in Canada?

"Pay to unlock your earnings" is a task scam in Canada because the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre lists unlock payments, upgrade fees, and upfront deposits as warning signs of job fraud, not as a step in real hiring. On the [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm), fraudsters offer simple tasks, show a rising balance, and then require a fee to reach the next level or to take the money out. The centre's advice is never to send deposits or pay fees for a job.

A real payer owes you money after the work. They do not sell you access to money they already claim is yours. If the only way to reach the balance is another deposit, the balance is the prop.

### How do boosting, survey, and fake-store task scams work in Canada?

Boosting, survey, and fake-store task scams in Canada follow the variations the CAFC publishes. The May 30, 2024 alert on the job page says the centre is seeing crypto job frauds that use the names of real Canadian companies. You are offered freelance work to "boost" products, apps, or videos with software the fraudsters created. After you install it, you get orders or tasks. A small payment or commission may arrive so the job looks real. Higher commissions, the page says, require fees to "move up a level." Funds sit in a crypto account you cannot withdraw.

The same page's web-survey and e-commerce section says offers show up in social ads and in WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram, Messenger, or TikTok messages. Online-store jobs ask you to "process orders" or "manage inventory," then demand larger deposits to upgrade the account, fix an order error, or unlock higher commissions. Web-survey jobs let you finish a few simple surveys, then claim there is a problem and ask for a deposit to continue. The page says victims see a fake balance and cannot withdraw it.

The centre's [payment-methods bulletin](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/03/bulletin-3-eng.htm) says fraudsters commonly request cryptocurrency for job fraud because those payments are harder to trace, and that crypto sent this way is very unlikely to be reversed.

| CAFC variation | What they ask you to do | What the page says happens next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crypto boosting | Install their software and complete tasks | A small commission, then fees to level up; crypto you cannot withdraw |
| Fake online store | Process orders for a commission | Deposits to upgrade, fix an "error," or unlock commissions |
| Web surveys | Finish a few paid surveys | A problem on the account, then a deposit to continue |
| Recruiting | Bring in other people for higher pay | The page calls this pyramid selling, a criminal offence to promote |

### What are the warning signs of a task scam in Canada?

The warning signs of a task scam in Canada are the list on the CAFC job page. Stop if you see any of these:

- The offer uses a web-based email address instead of a company domain.
- You did not apply for the job.
- You are asked to join a platform, download software, or do simple tasks for fast money.
- The job requires a deposit, an upgrade fee, or an unlock payment.
- A balance is on the screen and you cannot withdraw it.
- You are asked to recruit others.
- You are asked to receive or forward money through your bank account.
- The offer promises high pay for minimal work.

The job page also says not to share ID with unverified contacts, and to be careful with cryptocurrency because it usually cannot be reversed. Verify a company on its own website and by calling a phone number you looked up, not the number in the message.

### How is a real paid task different from a task scam in Canada?

A real paid task in Canada pays you for the task. It does not invoice you for the privilege of being paid. Mystery shopping companies that the [mystery shopping guide](/side-hustles/mystery-shopping-side-hustle-canada/) points to pay after a report. They do not send a cheque first and they do not charge a joining fee. [Odd-job apps](/side-hustles/odd-jobs-apps-side-hustle-canada/) pay for a job a client booked. A platform that only shows earnings inside its own screen, and blocks withdrawal until you deposit more, matches the CAFC's fake-balance description.

If someone asks you to receive e-transfers and forward them, including into crypto, the payment bulletin says you may be moving other victims' money and acting as a money mule. The job page's financial-agent variation says that can be investigated as money laundering. Decline it.

### Where do you report a task scam in Canada?

Report a task scam in Canada to your local police and to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre through the Report Cybercrime and Fraud website, or by phone at 1-888-495-8501. That is the instruction on the [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm). Tell your bank or the crypto platform if money already moved. Do not pay a further fee because a stranger says they can recover the first deposit. Recovery pitches are their own fraud type on the 2025 top-10 page.

This is not tax advice. A fake balance is not income. If a platform paid you real dollars for real work and you are unsure how to report it, use [how to report side hustle income](/side-hustles/report-side-hustle-income-cra-canada/) and [are rewards and bonuses taxable](/side-hustles/are-rewards-and-bonuses-taxable-canada/). Those pages are about money you actually received, not about a number on a scam dashboard.

## How do fake cheque and overpayment scams work in Canada?

Fake cheque and overpayment scams in Canada trick side hustlers into sending real money against a payment that does not stick. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre's [payment-methods bulletin](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/03/bulletin-3-eng.htm) says suspects send fraudulent cheques, ask victims to deposit them, and tell them to transfer the funds before the cheque has cleared. If the cheque returns, you repay the full amount. A marketplace version, on the [merchandise page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/merchandise-marchandises-eng.htm), is the buyer who "overpays" and wants the difference wired back.

### How do fake cheque scams target side hustlers in Canada?

Fake cheque scams in Canada arrive as a job or a gig, with the cheque first and the work as a story. The CAFC [job page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) describes three versions side hustlers run into.

**Counterfeit cheque jobs.** A text or email answers a resume posted online. The roles the page lists include caregiver, administrative assistant, data entry clerk, and mystery shopper. A counterfeit cheque comes with a story about why they need help cashing it. You are asked to cash it and return some of the funds.

**Mystery shopper cheques.** A notice says you were selected as a mystery shopper, quality control officer, or similar. The notice includes a cheque. You are told to deposit it and transfer a large part to a specific account. The page says you may also be told to buy gift cards and send the numbers "to confirm they work," and to fill in a survey at each step. The CAFC calls the surveys a prop. The cheque is fake and you owe the bank.

**Car wrapping.** An unsolicited message says you can earn $300 to $500 per week wrapping a vehicle with a company logo. If you reply, instructions and a contract are followed by a cheque. You deposit it, withdraw funds, and send money to a graphics company or for other fees. The page says you then learn the cheque is counterfeit and you are responsible for funds withdrawn. Those weekly figures are the pitch on the CAFC page, not a rate this site is quoting as pay.

The payment bulletin's advice is short: be cautious when accepting a cheque. It can bounce, and you repay the full amount. Money that your bank lets you withdraw is not proof the cheque is good.

### How do overpayment and fake e-transfer scams target sellers in Canada?

Overpayment and fake e-transfer scams in Canada target people who listed something for sale. The CAFC [merchandise page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/merchandise-marchandises-eng.htm) says fraudsters contact anyone selling goods on social media or online classifieds. If you posted an ad, they may claim to be out of town and offer to buy the item unseen.

The page's overpayment tactic: you receive more than the asking price, you are told to deposit the funds and wire the excess back immediately, and you later find the payment was fraudulent. The page says fraudsters use compromised bank accounts, fraudulent cheques, and stolen credit cards for this.

The same page's e-transfer tactic: a fake "etransfer" email or text asks you to accept a payment. It looks official. The purpose the page names is to steal banking credentials. A separate CAFC note, [protecting yourself online](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/10/protecting-yourself-protegez-vous-eng.htm), adds that the message can be a request for funds from your account, or a phishing message, and that sellers are asked to ship the item and send a tracking number before a fake "pending" payment is released. That note also says not to transfer funds, for example to a shipping company, in order to receive a payment for an item you are selling.

The October 2025 [merchandise habits page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2025/10/avoid-merchandise-evitez-eng.htm) repeats the overpayment definition in one line: the original payment bounces or is reversed, and the seller is left without the product and without the refunded money. Its practical advice is to beware of overpayments, beware of overseas buyers who will not see the product, pick up and pay in person when you can, and use a payment method with fraud protection, such as a credit card, when you are the buyer.

Job fraud, which includes the cheque jobs, was $50.6 million. Those are reported losses, not every incident in Canada.

### What should Canadian sellers and gig workers do instead?

Canadian sellers and gig workers should keep the money moving in one direction: the other person pays you, and you do not pay them to be paid. For a local sale, the merchandise habits page says to pick up and pay in person whenever possible. Do not wire an "extra" amount. Do not deposit a stranger's cheque. Do not click an e-transfer link that arrived from the buyer and asks for banking credentials or a QR-code confirmation. The online-protection note says QR codes sent to sellers "so you can receive payment" have been used to collect online-banking details.

If you are the buyer on a marketplace, pay in a way you can dispute, and be wary of prices the habits page describes as blowout sales. That is the other side of merchandise fraud: you pay and nothing arrives.

A real mystery-shop company does not start with a cheque. The comparison is in [mystery shopping](/side-hustles/mystery-shopping-side-hustle-canada/). Listing habits for local pickup are in [Facebook Marketplace flipping](/side-hustles/facebook-marketplace-flipping-side-hustle-canada/) and [Facebook Marketplace listing tips](/side-hustles/facebook-marketplace-listing-tips-canada/).

#### Illustrative example

**Illustrative example.** Suppose you listed a dresser for $80, an assumed price, not a market quote. A buyer sends a payment of $300 and asks you to forward $220 for a courier. That shape is the overpayment tactic on the merchandise page: more than the asking price, then an immediate transfer of the excess. If the $300 later reverses, the sketch leaves you short the $220 you sent and without a trustworthy payment for the dresser. Do not ship the dresser against that message. The $80 and $300 are assumptions so the arithmetic is visible. They are not CAFC statistics.

### What do you do if you already deposited the cheque in Canada?

If you already deposited the cheque in Canada, contact your bank, stop sending any further funds, and report the fraud. The job page tells targets to report to local police and to Report Cybercrime and Fraud, online or at 1-888-495-8501. The payment bulletin says the same, and it says you can report an attempt even if you did not lose money.

Do not send a second payment because the first one is "stuck." Do not give account access to someone offering to fix the cheque. If the bank asks you to repay the returned cheque, that request is the outcome the CAFC describes. This page cannot tell you what your bank will do on a particular deposit. Ask the bank.

Selling your own goods is still a tax question when the sale is real. A fraudulent payment that reverses is not income you keep. How genuine personal sales differ from reselling is [is selling on Kijiji or Facebook Marketplace taxable](/side-hustles/is-selling-on-kijiji-taxable-canada/).

## How do you report a side hustle scam in Canada?

Report a side hustle scam in Canada to your local police and to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, and tell the bank if money already left the account. The Competition Bureau's page on [how to report fraud and scams](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/fraud-and-scams/tips-and-advice/how-report-fraud-and-scams-canada) lists those steps, and it says the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre estimates that less than 5% of fraud victims report to law enforcement. The centre's [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) gives the same two doors: local police, and the Report Cybercrime and Fraud website or 1-888-495-8501.

### Where do you report a side hustle scam in Canada?

You report a side hustle scam to local police and to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. Use both. The police report is the local record. The centre collects reports from across the country. The Competition Bureau's reporting page says to call the police especially if you lost money. If nobody is in immediate danger, use the non-emergency number published on your police service's own website. There is no single national non-emergency number. Do not use 911 for a report you can file on the non-emergency line.

| 2 | Local police | A report, and the file number they give you |
| 3 | Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre | [reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca](https://reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca/) or 1-888-495-8501 |
| 4 | Bank, card, or payment app | Notice that the transfer was fraud, via the number on the card |
| 5 | The website or app | The site's own report-abuse or report-an-ad link |
| 6 | Competition Bureau, if the problem is a misleading ad | Complaint form or 1-800-348-5358 |

The centre is jointly managed by the RCMP, the Competition Bureau, and the Ontario Provincial Police, according to the Bureau's [February 2025 notice](https://www.canada.ca/en/competition-bureau/news/2025/02/fraud-prevention-month-to-focus-on-impersonation-fraud-one-of-the-fastest-growing-forms-of-fraud.html). That notice also says to report to the centre even when there was no financial loss.

### What should you write down before you call?

Write down the facts while the messages are still in the phone. The Competition Bureau's reporting page asks you to keep the name used, what they wanted, when it happened, the phone number, the exact web address, and copies of emails, texts, receipts, and cancelled cheques. Save a screenshot of the sales page, the checkout, and the payment confirmation. If you found the page through a search, note the words you searched.

Keep those files in one folder. The same page says they may be evidence. Do not send the folder to a person who contacts you later and offers to recover the money. The centre's [home page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/index-eng.htm) warns that fraudsters impersonate the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre and that the centre does not contact people to request money.

#### Illustrative example

This is a teaching list of what a report contains. It is not a real file and not a typical loss.

Suppose the message used the name "Alex," a number you wrote down, and a web page you screenshotted on a Tuesday. Suppose you sent an assumed $400 by e-transfer at 8:15 p.m. and the bank reference is on the receipt. The report is those four facts plus the screenshot. The $400 is an assumption so the list has a shape. It is not an average loss. Stop contact before you add a second payment to "unlock" the first one.

### How do you report the scam to police without calling 911?

Contact the police service for the place you live, or the place the money was taken from, using the non-emergency number or online form on that service's website. The Competition Bureau says reporting to local law enforcement tells police in your area that someone may be targeting local people, and that you should call the police if you lost money. Ask for a file number and write it down. You will want it for the bank and for the Anti-Fraud Centre report.

Municipal police and the RCMP split coverage by community. This guide does not list detachment numbers, because they change and they are local. Search the service's own site for "non-emergency" plus your city. If someone is in danger, or the situation is happening now and you need urgent help, call 911.

### How do you report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre?

Report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre online at [reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca](https://reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca/) or by phone at 1-888-495-8501. The job-scam page and the Competition Bureau's reporting page both give that phone number. File the report even if you only received the message and did not pay. The Bureau's 2025 notice says a report with no financial loss still goes to the centre.

Have the police file number if you already have one. Have the payment details if money moved. The centre does not take payment from you to investigate, and it does not call you to ask for a fee. Hang up on anyone who says they are the centre and then asks for money, gift cards, or remote access to your computer.

### When should you call the bank, the platform, and the Competition Bureau?

Call the financial institution as soon as money has moved. The Competition Bureau's step 4 says to notify the bank, credit union, card issuer, or payment service that transferred the money, and to tell them you are reporting fraud on the account. Use the number on the back of your card, in your statement, or in the official app. A number inside the scam text is part of the scam. The centre's [payment-methods bulletin](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2024/03/bulletin-3-eng.htm) is the page that explains why a cheque or e-transfer that looked real can still be reversed, and why you should talk to the institution that moved the funds.

If the offer lived on a website, a marketplace, or a social network, use that site's report link. The Bureau names Facebook, eBay, Kijiji, and dating sites as examples, and says the link is often called "report abuse" or "report an ad." An app with its own help form gets the same treatment: report the profile inside the app, and keep the confirmation.

Misleading advertising is a Competition Bureau matter even when it is not a theft. The reporting page says you can report false or misleading marketing with the Bureau's online complaint form or by telephone at 1-800-348-5358. A course page that invents earnings is that kind of report. The buying-side warning signs are in [how to spot a course funnel](/side-hustles/course-funnel-red-flags-canada/).

If the scam used your identity, the same Bureau page says to contact both national credit bureaus and ask for a fraud alert: Equifax at 1-888-836-6351 and TransUnion at 1-800-663-9980. If you think your social insurance number was stolen, it says to contact Service Canada at 1-866-274-2267. Investment scams can also go to your provincial securities regulator through the Canadian Securities Administrators, which the Bureau lists on that page.

### What should you stop doing after you report?

Stop paying. A second fee to release a refund is the same scam. Stop installing software the sender asks for. Tell anyone whose name or photo was used, if you can do that without sending them money. Money you lost is not side hustle income. A small real payment for real work, if there was any, is a separate question in [how to report side hustle income](/side-hustles/report-side-hustle-income-cra-canada/). This page does not tell you how to deduct a fraud loss.

This is not legal advice.
