AI consulting for small businesses in Canada means helping an owner use AI tools on real, repeated work: drafting replies, summarizing documents, sorting email, writing proposals, building simple automations, and training staff to use them safely. Most small businesses haven't started yet: Statistics Canada found 12.2% of businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services in the year to spring 2025, so a consultant who can find one useful task and set it up properly has plenty of room to work.

<QuickAnswer>
- **Demand:** 12.2% of Canadian businesses used AI in the 12 months before Statistics Canada's Q2 2025 survey, up from 6.1% a year earlier; 18.2% of those users worked with vendors or consultants to install or integrate it, up from 11.3%.
- **Published Canadian prices:** Vancouver's Signal & Form lists a $1,500 workflow audit, $2,500 readiness assessment and $5,000 to $25,000 automation builds; Vernon, B.C.'s Jarvis Strategies starts at a $300 opportunity scan (both checked October 9, 2026).

- **Startup cost:** $0 to about $30 a month: a laptop you own, a paid AI chat plan if you want one, and free tiers of Zapier (100 tasks a month) or Make.
- **Rules and tax:** follow the Privacy Commissioner's generative AI principles and don't overstate results; income goes on T2125 with both CPP shares, and GST/HST applies past $30,000.

## Who does AI consulting suit?

It suits people who already understand how a particular kind of business runs and can use AI tools well enough to show an owner a working result in their own documents. Industry knowledge sells better than technical depth: an owner wants someone who knows what a change order, a dental recall list or a property showing request is.

Good fits:

- **Office managers, bookkeepers and executive assistants**, who know where admin hours go.
- **Tradespeople and project managers**, who can speak to estimating, scheduling and site paperwork.
- **Marketers and writers**, who can build content and email workflows.
- **Developers and analysts**, who can wire up automations and reporting.
- **Retired managers**, who bring credibility with owners their own age.

It's a poor fit if you'd paste a client's customer list into a free consumer chat tool, can't explain what a tool does with the data, or want to sell guaranteed time savings.

## How much demand is there in Canada?

Real, but early. Statistics Canada's Canadian Survey on Business Conditions (collected April 1 to May 5, 2025; 9,103 responding businesses out of a sample of 21,357; released June 16, 2025) found:

| Measure (Q2 2025) | Share of businesses |
|---|---|
| Used AI to produce goods or deliver services in the past 12 months | 12.2% (6.1% in Q2 2024) |
| Plan to adopt AI software in the next 12 months | 17.9% (11.5% in Q2 2024) |
| Consider AI investment "not relevant" | 41.2% |
| Highest use: information and cultural industries | 35.6% |
| Professional, scientific and technical services | 31.7% |
| Lowest use: accommodation and food services | 1.5% |

Among businesses already using AI, the most common applications were text analytics (35.7%), data analytics (26.4%), virtual agents or chatbots (24.8%) and marketing automation (23.1%). After adopting AI, 40.1% developed new workflows, 38.9% trained current staff, and 18.2% worked with vendors or consulting services to install or integrate it. Those last three are exactly what an AI consultant sells: workflow design, training and setup.

Two practical takeaways: professional-service firms (accountants, lawyers, engineers, agencies) are the most receptive buyers, and restaurants and trades are the least adopted, which means more untouched work but more persuading.

- Time saved: 4 × $26.44 = $105.76 a week; × 48 weeks = $5,076.48 a year
- Tool cost: $28.47 × 12 = $341.64 a year

Put this math in your proposal with the owner's own numbers. If you can't estimate hours saved before you start, sell the audit first.

17. **Any owner with spreadsheets:** AI-summarized weekly reports from existing tools.

## What privacy and advertising rules apply?

Two federal sources set the guardrails for most private-sector clients.

**Privacy.** The Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada and provincial and territorial regulators published [principles for responsible, trustworthy and privacy-protective generative AI](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/technology/artificial-intelligence/gd_principles_ai) on December 7, 2023. They cover legal authority and consent, appropriate purposes, necessity and proportionality, openness, accountability, individual access, limiting collection, use and disclosure, accuracy and safeguards. In practice for a small-business setup:

- Don't put customer personal information into a tool unless the client has a lawful basis and the tool's business terms fit.
- Prefer business or enterprise plans with admin controls over personal free accounts.
- Keep a human review step before anything AI-drafted reaches a customer.
- Write down what data each automation touches. The [PIPEDA fair information principles](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/p_principle/) and the OPC's [privacy guide for businesses](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/pipeda-compliance-help/guide_org/) are the baseline.

Health, legal and financial clients may have extra professional or provincial obligations; leave regulated records out of scope unless the client's compliance lead signs off.

**Advertising.** The Competition Bureau's page on [false or misleading representations](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/deceptive-marketing-practices/types-deceptive-marketing-practices/false-or-misleading-representations) applies to your own marketing. "Saves 10 hours a week" is a claim; only make it if you measured it, and say for whom.

## How do you start? A 30-day plan

### Week 1: Pick a niche and build two demos

- Choose one client type you understand.
- Build two demos on made-up data: for example, a proposal drafted from three sample past proposals, and an inbox rule that drafts replies to the five most common questions.
- Record a 3-minute screen video of each.

### Week 2: Write your offer and policies

- Write a one-page offer: the audit, one build, the price, what's out of scope.
- Write your data rules (what you will and won't put into which tool) and a short AI-use policy template for clients.
- Set up scheduling and invoicing.

### Week 3: Talk to ten owners

- Message ten owners in your niche you already know or can be introduced to (template below).
- Offer a 30-minute free call; ask where the repeated hours go.
- Sell the audit, not the build.

### Week 4: Deliver and document

- Deliver your first audit with a measured baseline (hours per week on the task).
- Propose one build with the payback math.
- Ask for permission to describe the result anonymously.

### Days 31–90: Builds and a retainer

- Deliver builds with a training session and a written runbook.
- Offer a small monthly retainer for tweaks and new prompts.
- In B.C., employers can apply to the [B.C. Employer Training Grant](https://www.workbc.ca/employers-industry/funding-programs/bc-employer-training-grant), which reimburses 80% of eligible training costs up to $10,000 per participant per fiscal year; the employer applies and eligibility is decided by the program.

## Templates you can copy

### Outreach message

```
Hi [name], I help [client type] in [city] use AI tools on the repetitive
parts of the week: [example 1, e.g. proposal first drafts] and
[example 2, e.g. replies to common emails]. I start with a fixed-price
audit ($[price]) that maps where the hours go and recommends one change.
Would a 30-minute call next week be useful?
```

### Audit deliverable outline

```
1. The task list: each repeated task, who does it, hours per week (measured)
2. Top 3 opportunities, ranked by hours saved and risk
3. What data each would touch, and the tool/plan recommended
4. What NOT to automate, and why
5. One recommended first build: scope, price, payback estimate
6. Staff training and AI-use policy recommendations
```

### Scope and data checklist for every build

- The exact task, inputs and outputs.
- Which tool and plan, on whose account (the client's, never yours).
- What personal information is involved; if any, the client's sign-off.
- The human review step before anything reaches a customer.
- Baseline hours and how you'll measure after 30 days.
- Handover: login ownership, runbook, training session.

### Client AI-use policy starter

```
- Use only the company-approved AI tools and accounts.
- Don't paste customer personal information, passwords or financial
  account numbers into AI tools unless the tool is approved for it.
- A person reviews every AI draft before it is sent or published.
- Tell the manager if an AI output is wrong or inappropriate.
```

## What mistakes do new AI consultants make?

- **Selling "AI" instead of an outcome.** Owners buy fewer hours on proposals, not a chatbot.
- **Skipping the baseline.** Without before-hours you can't prove the after.
- **Building on your own accounts.** The client must own every login and subscription.
- **Ignoring data rules.** One customer list in the wrong tool can end the relationship.
- **Automating judgment.** Hiring, pricing, legal and medical decisions stay with people.
- **Overpromising.** Claims about savings fall under the Competition Bureau's misleading-representation rules.
- **No handover.** Leave a runbook and train a staff member, or the build dies when you leave.

## Which tax rules apply to AI consulting in Canada?

Consulting income is self-employment income. Report it on [Form T2125, Statement of Business or Professional Activities](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html) and pay both the employee and employer shares of [Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html) contributions on net income. Deductible costs include software subscriptions you pay for the business, a share of internet and phone, a portion of your computer through capital cost allowance, and professional development.

GST/HST follows the [$30,000 small-supplier test](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html): worldwide taxable supplies, yours and your associates', of $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Count other side-hustle revenue too. Consulting isn't ride-sharing, so the first-dollar rule on the CRA's [taxi and ride-sharing](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-specific-situations/taxi-ride-sharing-drivers.html) page doesn't apply. Once registered, charge the rate for the province where the supply is made; see the CRA's [charge and collect the GST/HST](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate.html) table and [GST/HST calculator (and rates)](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate/calculator.html). For a set-aside habit, see [how much tax to set aside for a side hustle in Canada](/side-hustles/how-much-tax-set-aside-side-hustle-canada/).

This is not tax, legal or insurance advice.

### Provincial notes

- **British Columbia:** client employers may use the B.C. Employer Training Grant for eligible training; PST rules for software and services are separate from GST, so check with the province.
- **Ontario:** HST is 13% once registered.
- **Nova Scotia:** HST is 14% as of April 1, 2025 on the CRA table.
- **Quebec:** QPP replaces CPP, and QST applies alongside GST once registered; Quebec's private-sector privacy law also applies to clients there, so check with Revenu Québec and the Commission d'accès à l'information.
- **Alberta:** 5% GST only once registered; Alberta and B.C. have their own private-sector privacy laws.

## What should you keep in mind about these numbers?

The Statistics Canada figures describe businesses with employees in spring 2025 and are as published on June 16, 2025. Job Bank wages are for employees in 2023–2024, not freelancers. Consultancy prices are as listed on the firms' sites on October 9, 2026; Jarvis Strategies' client results are its own anonymized reports, and BCBusiness's Overdrive figures are as reported on March 30, 2026. None are typical results. Software prices change and Zapier lists USD by default; the conversion uses the Bank of Canada rate of 1.4240 on October 8, 2026. Prices, hours, time saved and the yearly scenarios in the worked examples are assumptions. Privacy obligations depend on the client's sector and province.
