## The quick answer

Ranges are typical Canadian retail and used prices, to verify locally. They are not quotes. They assume you can store a shovel and a bag of melt, and that you are not buying a truck.

| How you start | One-time cash, about | What you can clear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lean shovel route | $90–$220 | An ergonomic shovel, a smaller one for steps, a bag or two of ice melt, a headlamp, and boots you already trust on ice. You have asked about liability insurance even if the quote is still in your inbox. |
| Typical neighbourhood start | $200–$450 | Better boots if you needed them, a second bag of melt, spare lamp batteries, and gloves. Still no machine. |
| Doing it properly | $800–$2,500 | The shovel kit, boots you would wear at minus 20, a liability quote you accepted, a salt stock, and a used single-stage blower only after the route is real. A new two-stage machine is an upgrade for deep snow and gravel, often $1,500–$3,500, and it is the wrong first purchase. |

Canada does not have one winter. Prairie, Ottawa, Montreal, and much of Atlantic Canada can support a season. Southern Ontario away from the snowbelts is variable. A mild coastal B.C. street should not finance a blower. Price the street you are on, including a winter that barely comes.

## Who this budget fits

**It fits** if you can get up early, you are steady on your feet, and snow where you live is a repeating event. It fits lawn clients who already trust you. The lean column fits a walkable set of driveways you could finish with a shovel before work.

**It is a poor fit** if you have a heart or back history you are hoping will “be fine,” if you cannot leave a day job on storm mornings, or if you want passive income. It is a poor fit if the only plan is a financed machine and no signed driveways.

## Ongoing costs, and a simple break-even

**Ice melt.** The repeating supply. An icy year burns bags. Store it dry. Pet-safer melt is not candy. Scrape first, then follow the bag rate. Salt piled on uncleared snow wastes money and hurts concrete and paws.

**Fuel and shear pins**, only if you own a blower. A shovel route’s fuel bill is your breakfast and your boots.

**Vehicle.** $0 if you walk the route. If you drive, log the kilometres and set aside wear. A plough windrow at the end of the driveway is not a fuel question. Your agreement should say whether a return visit is included, extra, or not your job.

**Insurance.** The seasonal premium. It does not shrink in a green January.

**Unpaid scope.** Roofs and ice dams injure people, and casual insurance will not treat them as a shovel job. Free sidewalks all winter destroy the price.

**Weather risk.** Per-visit pay dies in a mild winter. A seasonal contract with no trigger depth and no cap dies in a harsh one. Write the trigger in centimetres before the snow.

### How to calculate break-even

1. One-time gear (shovel kit, or shovel kit plus blower).
2. Seasonal fixed: insurance, invoicing software, a base stock of melt.
3. Variable per visit: extra melt, blower fuel, kilometres, the time.

For a seasonal contract, contribution for the winter = total fees collected minus variable costs for the visits you actually did.

Gear is earned back when contribution covers the startup. If a heavy winter pays less per hour than a hard day-job shift, rewrite the next client’s cap. Honour the written deal for the current one. Sketch a light, typical, and heavy winter. A machine payment that a light year cannot carry is not affordable.

### Illustrative example

**A teaching sketch, not a contract price and not a snowfall forecast.**

Shovel, lamp, two bags of melt, and batteries cost $180. You already own boots. Liability insurance in this sketch is $200 for the season. You sign four small driveways at $350 each for the season, trigger at 5 cm, windrow return included, salt included. Fees = $1,400. You bought two more bags of melt at $30, so $60. You walked. No blower fuel.

Variable and seasonal supplies beyond the opening bags = $60. Insurance $200. Contribution after those = $1,400 − $60 − $200 = $1,140. Startup gear $180 is covered inside the first seasonal cheques in this sketch, with a large remainder.

Hour test: a light winter of 6 visits versus a heavy one of 18. Four driveways times 18 visits is 72 clears. At 25 minutes each, the heavy year is 30 hours and this sketch is $1,140 ÷ 30 = $38 an hour. The light year is only 10 hours, which is why the cap belongs in the October agreement. A financed $2,000 blower on a winter of three visits flips the sketch. Do not copy $350.

## Where you can spend less

Shovel first. Buy the blower when the morning is too long, from a seller who will start it in the cold. Walk a tight route. A sled beats a trailer.

Buy one pet-safer melt before the rush, and store it dry. Use a notebook until the street is real. Do not add roof clearing. Write the mild-winter rule in October: retainer, visit cap, or a small credit.

## Where not to cut corners

Liability insurance before seasonal money changes hands. Home policies often exclude business. Slip-and-fall is the serious case. This is not insurance advice. Ask a broker while they are still answering October calls.

Boots and a lamp. Cheap smooth soles and a dead light are how people get hurt in a driveway they were paid to make safe.

The city’s rule on where snow may go. Do not push it into the street, onto a hydrant, a bus stop, or a neighbour’s cleared walk. A fine or a complaint is not a rounding error. Read the winter page.

Shear-pin discipline if you graduate to a blower. Turn the machine off and pull the key before you clear a clog. Fingers do not grow back.

Your body. Cancel a stop with black ice you cannot manage. A blower payment is not a reason to keep an unsafe driveway.

## Canada note

This is not tax, legal, or insurance advice.

Snow income is self-employment income, including a seasonal lump sum. A deposit in November is still income to track. Keep the agreements, the e-transfers, and the receipts for salt, fuel, and repairs. Report it on form T2125 with your T1. Net self-employment income generally means both shares of CPP.

**Log and CCA.** If you drive, keep a kilometre log: date, destination, kilometres, purpose. Claim the business portion only. Shovels, melt, and fuel are ordinary expenses. A blower is usually capital. Capital cost allowance (CCA) recovers that cost over time on the business portion, in the class the CRA assigns, not necessarily in full on the September receipt. Ask an accountant if the machine is a large part of the claim.

**GST/HST.** The small-supplier test is worldwide taxable supplies, yours and your associates’, of $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Add lawn, cleaning, and handyman revenue to the same pot. The CRA explains the timing on its [when to register](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html) page. Snow removal is not ride-sharing, so the taxi first-dollar rule does not apply unless you also carry passengers for a platform.

**Input tax credits.** If you are registered, GST/HST paid on melt, fuel, and equipment for the business can often be claimed as an input tax credit to the extent of commercial use. Keep receipts that show the tax. If you are not registered, you do not charge the tax and you do not claim the credit.

Once registered, charge the provincial rate. Some provinces use HST. Others add PST on top of GST. Quebec charges GST and QST. Nova Scotia’s HST is 14% as of 1 April 2025. Use the CRA [rate table](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate/calculator.html) and set the tax aside. A seasonal deposit is not spending money until you have separated any tax you owe.

Your contract does not erase negligence. Search your city for a business licence and the sidewalk deadline. If you pay a helper, check that province’s workers’ compensation board first. Store fuel the way the can and your municipality describe.
