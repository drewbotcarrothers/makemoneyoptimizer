## Who does selling online courses in Canada suit?

Selling online courses in Canada suits people who can explain a specific skill step by step, have proof they can do it, and can reach the people who need it, through a blog, newsletter, social following, or professional network. It's a poor fit if you'd be learning the topic as you teach it, if you don't enjoy recording or writing, or if you need income in the next month.

### Illustrative example

This is a teaching sketch with assumed numbers, not a forecast. Suppose you sell a mini-course for an assumed $49 on Payhip's free plan. Payhip's 5% fee is $49 × 0.05 = $2.45, and payment processing is extra (check Stripe or PayPal's current rate). If 20 people buy in a year, platform fees are 20 × $2.45 = $49, versus 12 × about $56 = about $672 for a year of Teachable Starter before its 7.5% fee. At low volume, a free plan is cheaper; at higher volume, the math can flip.

## How do you choose an online course topic in Canada?

Choose an online course topic in Canada by finding a narrow problem people already ask you about, then checking that they'd pay to solve it. Good signals: repeated questions from clients or followers, a skill that saves time or money, and a Canadian angle that generic courses miss, such as CRA record keeping for freelancers or setting up a Canadian Shopify store. Sell it before building it: a pre-sale or a waitlist with a few paying buyers is the best validation.

## What rules apply to marketing an online course in Canada?

Marketing an online course in Canada has to be truthful. The Competition Bureau's page on [false or misleading representations](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/deceptive-marketing-practices/types-deceptive-marketing-practices/false-or-misleading-representations) says the Competition Act prohibits promoting a product through claims that are false or misleading in any material respect, and that offers using words like "free" must clearly disclose all conditions. Avoid income or results guarantees you can't back up, and make refund terms clear.

Copyright protects your course materials automatically: CIPO's [guide to copyright](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/guide-copyright) says a work is protected the moment it is created and fixed in a material form. That cuts both ways, so only use images, music, and excerpts you have the right to use.

## How do you start selling an online course in Canada?

Starting an online course in Canada means validating one narrow topic, building a short version, and improving it with real student feedback.

### Step 1: Pick one problem and one audience

"Set up bookkeeping for a Canadian side hustle in a weekend" beats "Business basics."

### Step 2: Validate with a pre-sale or waitlist

Share an outline and price with your audience. If nobody commits, rethink before recording.

### Step 3: Outline five to ten short lessons

Each lesson should end with the student able to do one thing.

### Step 4: Record simply

Clear audio, screen recordings or a phone on a stand, and short videos. Add a worksheet per lesson.

### Step 5: Choose a platform

Start on a free plan (Gumroad or Payhip) unless you need course-specific features from day one.

### Step 6: Launch to your existing audience

Email, social posts, or a [newsletter](/side-hustles/newsletter-side-hustle-canada/). Cold launches with no audience rarely sell.

### Step 7: Update from feedback

Ask first students where they got stuck and fix those lessons first.

## What mistakes are common when selling online courses in Canada?

Common online course mistakes in Canada are building too much before validating, paying for an expensive platform too early, and over-promising results.

- **A huge first course.** Ship a mini-course first.
- **No audience.** Build an email list or following alongside the course.
- **Platform first, buyers later.** Monthly fees add up with no sales.
- **Results promises.** Misleading claims can breach the Competition Act.
- **Using content you don't own.** Clear rights for music, images, and templates.

## Which tax rules apply to online course income in Canada?

Online course income in Canada is generally self-employment income, reported on [Form T2125](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html) with expenses such as platform fees, equipment, and software. GST/HST registration becomes mandatory once taxable sales pass the [$30,000 small-supplier threshold](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html). Gumroad says it acts as merchant of record and handles sales tax where it has obligations; check how your platform handles tax. See [GST/HST registration](/side-hustles/gst-hst-registration-side-hustle-canada/) and [how to report side hustle income](/side-hustles/report-side-hustle-income-cra-canada/).
