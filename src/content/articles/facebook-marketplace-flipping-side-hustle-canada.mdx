Facebook Marketplace flipping in Canada means buying underpriced local items (tools, small furniture, bikes, phones, kitchen gear), cleaning or fixing them and reselling them locally for more. Marketplace is where Canadians already look first: a 2025 DIG360 and Angus Reid Institute poll found 77% of adults bought at least one pre-owned item in the past year, and Facebook Marketplace was the most popular place to buy. The work is mostly sourcing, messaging and meetups, so profit per hour depends on picking one category you know.

## Who does Marketplace flipping suit?

It suits people who already know what one category of item is worth, can test it before paying, have a vehicle and some storage, and are comfortable meeting strangers in public. Every flip involves two meetups, one to buy and one to sell, and many more messages.

Good fits:

- **Handy people** who can fix a bike, replace a battery or tighten a wobbly chair.
- **Tech-savvy sellers** who can check a phone's battery health, lock status and IMEI on the spot.
- **People with a truck, trailer or hitch**, since large items are often underpriced when sellers can't deliver them.
- **Parents and hobbyists** who know prices in kids' gear, sports equipment or tools because they buy it anyway.

It's a poor fit if you can't spare evenings for messages, have nowhere to store items, or would rather not lift heavy things.

## What are Meta's rules for selling on Marketplace in Canada?

Meta's [commerce policies](https://www.facebook.com/policies_center/commerce) apply to every Marketplace listing, including free items. They describe Marketplace as intended for consumer-to-consumer sales and prohibit, among other things, recalled or banned products, stolen goods or items acquired without authorization, animals, alcohol, medical and healthcare products, and counterfeits. Listings that break the policies can be removed, and repeated violations can lose you access to commerce features.

Two rules matter most for flippers. Don't buy anything that looks stolen, such as a new tool with the serial number removed or a bike sold far below value with no story. And check recalls before reselling anything, especially children's products.

Meta's help page on [getting paid for selling with shipping](https://www.facebook.com/help/449101635835192) says some shipping features aren't available to all sellers. This guide doesn't state a Canada-wide Marketplace fee for local pickup; check the help centre from your own account.

## How much can you make flipping on Marketplace?

There's no official average, so start from public examples and your own log.

- **A tracked beginner test.** Winston Sih wrote in Moose Money on March 6, 2026 that he spent about 20 hours buying, listing, messaging and meeting buyers in Toronto. A Nintendo Switch bought for $300 sold for $330 ($30 profit); a nightstand bought for $78 sold for $145 ($67 profit); a $157 Aesop soap set didn't sell. Totals: $378 spent on the two sold items, $475 received, $97 profit, about $4.85 an hour.
- **A phone flipper's week.** Supercar Blondie reported on April 30, 2026 that Canadian YouTuber Kristian Kumric bought an iPhone 13 for $300 expecting to sell near $399, an iPhone 13 Pro with a cracked screen and camera lens for $175 that he estimated could sell for up to $349 as-is or about $500 after roughly $65 in repairs, and an iPhone 12 Pro talked down from $250 to $200. He found the first phone was a weak deal once selling fees were counted.
- **A specialist at scale.** Daily Hive reported on August 17, 2024 that Tim MacLeod, a registered practical nurse in Muskoka, Ont., moved from flipping phones to buying and reselling couches, mostly leather sectionals. He reported more than 2,000 transactions and $670,000 in sales over five years.

What they share: profit came from knowing one category's prices, and the beginner test shows that time spent messaging is the biggest cost. Ontario's general minimum wage is $17.95 an hour from October 1, 2026, a useful bar for whether a flip was worth your evening.

## What does the math look like on a flip?

**Worked example 1, a cordless drill flip in Toronto (illustrative).** Fuel uses Statistics Canada's August 2026 Toronto average of 170.3¢/L. The prices, kilometres, 9 L/100 km fuel use and time are assumptions.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Buy price | $40.00 |
| Cleaning supplies | $5.00 |
| Pickup drive, 20 km (20 × 0.09 L × $1.703) | $3.07 |
| Sale price at a public meetup | $85.00 |
| Profit before tax | $36.93 |
| Time (messages, pickup, cleaning, listing, meetup) | 2.5 hours |
| Profit per hour | $14.77 |

If the battery is dead and a replacement costs an assumed $30, profit falls to $6.93, or $2.77 an hour. Testing at pickup is the cheapest insurance you have.

**Worked example 2, selling the same drill on eBay.ca instead (illustrative).** eBay's fee is 13.6% of the total amount of the sale, including shipping and sales tax, plus $0.40 per order over $10. At $85 plus 13% Ontario HST collected at checkout ($96.05 total), the fee is $96.05 × 13.6% + $0.40 = $13.46, before shipping. Local Marketplace pickup avoids that fee and the shipping cost, which is why bulky items stay local.

**Worked example 3, a month of weekend flips (illustrative).** Eight flips a month at worked example 1's $36.93 is $295.44 profit for 20 hours, about $14.77 an hour. One dud a month at a $40 loss drops that to $218.51, or $10.93 an hour. That gap is why a log matters.

## How do you start? A 30-day plan

### Week 1: Rules and your own stuff

- Read the [commerce policies](https://www.facebook.com/policies_center/commerce), [meeting-in-person tips](https://www.facebook.com/help/2329750133711372?locale=en_GB) and [scams page](https://www.facebook.com/help/721562085854101/). Write down what you won't sell.
- Photograph five unused items in daylight and list them with a pickup area, not your address.

### Week 2: Pick one category and learn prices

- Choose one category you can test (tools, bikes, consoles or small furniture).
- Track 20 asking prices and any sold prices you can find in a sheet.
- Finish your own-item sales; note how fast each sold and for how much.

### Week 3: First two flips

- Set a maximum buy price for each item before you message the seller.
- Meet in public, test, check serials and recalls, then pay.
- Clean, photograph and list honestly within 48 hours.

### Week 4: Review

- Work out profit per hour for each flip, including fuel and time.
- Keep the category if it beat your target; switch if not.
- Decide on a small flip float only if both flips made money.

## What mistakes do new flippers make?

- **Buying before learning prices.** Sell your own items first and track sold prices.
- **Skipping tests at pickup.** A dead battery can wipe out the profit.
- **Trusting payment screenshots.** Facebook's scams page warns against them; confirm cash or a landed e-Transfer in your bank app before handing over the item.
- **Accepting overpayment.** Overpayment and fake-cheque patterns are covered in [fake cheque and overpayment scams](/side-hustles/fake-cheque-overpayment-scams-canada/).
- **Buying items that look stolen.** It breaks Meta's policies and can cost you the item.
- **Reselling recalled children's products.** It shifts a safety risk onto a buyer.
- **Giving out your home address.** Facebook's meetup tips advise against it.

If you're targeted, report it to the [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://www.antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/index-eng.htm), which counted 2,596 merchandise fraud reports and $11.7 million in reported losses in 2025.

## What do Canada's safety and advertising rules require?

Health Canada's [second-hand products guide](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/consumer-product-safety/reports-publications/industry-professionals/industry-guide-second-hand-products-including-children-products/guidance-document.html) says dealers have primary responsibility for the safety of what they sell, that the law doesn't separate new and used products, and that recalled items that haven't been corrected shouldn't be sold. Search [recalls and safety alerts](https://recalls-rappels.canada.ca/en) before you buy or resell. Skip cribs, car seats and hockey helmets as early flips.

Describe items honestly. The Competition Bureau's page on [false or misleading representations](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/deceptive-marketing-practices/types-deceptive-marketing-practices/false-or-misleading-representations) covers claims made to sell a product.

## Which tax rules apply to Marketplace flipping in Canada?

Buying items to resell for profit is generally business income, reported on [Form T2125, Statement of Business or Professional Activities](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html), and self-employed people pay both the employee and employer [Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html) contributions on net income. Keep purchase records or screenshots, sale records, supply receipts and a kilometre log; the cost of items sold, supplies and the business share of vehicle costs are deductible.

Selling your own used belongings is a different picture from flipping for profit. The personal-use property rule and platform reporting are covered in [tax on reselling, gig apps and platform income in Canada](/side-hustles/tax-reselling-gig-apps-platform-income-canada/), and the Kijiji and Marketplace version of that split is [is selling on Kijiji taxable](/side-hustles/is-selling-on-kijiji-taxable-canada/).

GST/HST follows the [$30,000 small-supplier test](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html): worldwide taxable supplies, yours and your associates', of $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Count other side-hustle revenue too. Flipping isn't ride-sharing, so the first-dollar rule on the CRA's [taxi and ride-sharing](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-specific-situations/taxi-ride-sharing-drivers.html) page doesn't apply unless you also carry passengers for a platform. Once registered, charge the rate for the province where the supply is made; see the CRA's [charge and collect the GST/HST](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate.html) table and [GST/HST calculator (and rates)](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate/calculator.html). For a set-aside habit, see [how much tax to set aside for a side hustle in Canada](/side-hustles/how-much-tax-set-aside-side-hustle-canada/).

This is not tax, legal or insurance advice.

### Provincial notes

- **Ontario:** HST is 13% once registered. The $17.95 minimum wage is a fair yardstick for your profit per hour.
- **Nova Scotia:** HST is 14% as of April 1, 2025 on the CRA table.
- **Quebec:** QPP replaces CPP, and QST applies alongside GST once registered; check Revenu Québec.
- **British Columbia, Saskatchewan and Manitoba:** provincial sales tax runs separately from GST; check your province's rules before registering for PST.
- **Alberta:** 5% GST only once registered.
