## Who does event photography in Canada suit?

Event photography in Canada suits people who can anticipate moments, ask a group of strangers to squeeze in, and keep shooting when the light is bad. It is a poor fit if you freeze in crowds, if you can't commit to evenings and weekends, or if you dislike the hours of culling and editing after the event.

Unlike a portrait session, an event doesn't pause for you. The cake gets cut once, the diploma is handed over once, and the speech happens once. Preparation, backup gear, and knowing the schedule are what make the gallery complete.

## Who owns event photos in Canada?

Under [section 13 of the Copyright Act](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-42/page-3.html), the author of a work is generally the first owner of the copyright, and the old subsection about commissioned photographs has been repealed. Work made in the course of employment is different: the employer generally owns it unless there's an agreement to the contrary.

In practice, write down what the client may do with the photos: personal use, social media, marketing, or print. If a business wants to use event photos in ads, say so in the agreement. If you want to use the photos in your portfolio, ask permission in the agreement too, especially when children or private gatherings are involved. This is not legal advice.

## How much can event photography earn in Canada?

Event photography earnings in Canada depend on the event, the hours of coverage, and how long editing takes, so this guide does not publish a national rate. What you keep depends on:

- Hours on site and travel time
- The number of edited images promised, and the turnaround

### Illustrative example

This is a teaching sketch with assumed numbers, not a quote or a market rate. Suppose a two-hour birthday is priced at an assumed $250. Suppose the job takes 2 hours on site, 1 hour of travel, and 3 hours of culling and editing: 2 + 1 + 3 = 6 hours, and $250 ÷ 6 ≈ $41.67 an hour before gear, software, and a tax set-aside. If editing balloons to 6 hours, the total is 9 hours and $250 ÷ 9 ≈ $27.78 an hour. Editing time is where event pricing usually goes wrong.

## How do you start event photography in Canada?

Starting event photography in Canada means practising at low-stakes events, writing a simple agreement, and booking small paid events before weddings. The steps below follow that order.

### Step 1: Shoot two free events

A friend's party, a community group, or a school club event. Practise indoor light, group shots, and candid moments, and time how long editing takes.

### Step 2: Build a short portfolio

Twenty to thirty of your best images across two or three event types. Ask permission before showing identifiable people, especially children.

### Step 3: Write a one-page agreement

Include date, hours, location, price, deposit, delivery time, number of edited images, cancellation terms, and what the client may do with the photos under [the Copyright Act](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-42/page-3.html) ownership rule. This is not legal advice.

### Step 4: Prepare a backup kit

Two batteries, two cards, a cleaning cloth, and a second lens if you have one. Check the venue's lighting and rules in advance.

### Step 5: Get a shot list from the client

Who must be photographed, which moments matter (cake, speeches, awards), and the schedule. A five-minute call prevents a missed toast.

### Step 6: Offer small events locally

Tell parents' groups, community associations, small businesses, and venues. Graduation season and the holiday party season create bursts of demand.

### Step 7: Deliver on time and ask for a review

Back up the files the same day, deliver the gallery by the promised date, and invoice. Ask the client for a short testimonial you can share.

## What mistakes are common in event photography in Canada?

Common event photography mistakes in Canada are a single memory card, no written agreement, and starting with weddings. Each one puts a once-only moment at risk.

- **One card, one battery.** Hardware fails at the worst moment. Carry spares and swap cards during long events.
- **No agreement on usage.** Spell out personal versus commercial use, and whether you may use images in your portfolio.
- **Promising too many images.** A big number sounds generous until you're editing at 2 a.m.
- **Starting with weddings.** Weddings are high-stakes and long. Build experience at smaller events first.
- **Underpricing editing time.** Track the hours after the event, not only the hours on site.

## Which tax rules apply to event photography in Canada?

Event photography income in Canada is self-employment income reported on [Form T2125, Statement of Business or Professional Activities](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html), and self-employed people pay both halves of the [Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html) contribution on net self-employment income. GST/HST follows the [$30,000 small-supplier test](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html) across all your side-hustle revenue.

This is not tax, legal, or insurance advice.

Once registered, charge the rate for the province on the CRA's [charge and collect the GST/HST](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate.html) page. Cameras and lenses that last for years are usually claimed through [capital cost allowance](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/sole-proprietorships-partnerships/report-business-income-expenses/claiming-capital-cost-allowance.html) rather than in full in the year you buy them; memory cards, software, and travel to events can be expenses when you keep receipts.
