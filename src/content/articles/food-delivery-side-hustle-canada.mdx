## Who is food delivery a good fit for in Canada?

Food delivery in Canada fits people who already have a reliable vehicle, or who live where bike delivery is actually offered, and who can work evenings, weekends, and bad weather. ## How do you start food delivery in Canada?

Starting food delivery in Canada means confirming a live zone, calling the insurer about your own province, and applying to one app before extra gear. The steps below are that sequence.

### Step 1: Check that the apps are live on your streets

Install Skip, Uber Eats, and DoorDash and look at the map around home and around the neighbourhood where you would actually drive. A city name on a marketing page is not the same as offers on your block. If none of the three show a delivery zone, do not buy bags yet.

### Step 2: Call your insurer before you upload documents

Say plainly that you want to do food delivery in the personal vehicle, name the apps, and ask whether the current policy covers food delivery. Ask what happens while you are waiting for an offer, while you are driving to the restaurant, and while the food is in the car. Ask what endorsement or policy they need in writing.

[Uber delivery driver insurance in Canada](https://www.uber.com/ca/en/deliver/insurance/) says Uber maintains a commercial auto policy, issued by Economical, while you are available for a delivery, and that this does not replace your own personal policy. The same page has described many basic ICBC policies in British Columbia as allowing up to six days a month of commercial use. That six-day line is Uber’s description, not a reading of an ICBC statute, and it is not a promise that your certificate already says it. Read the certificate.

DoorDash’s requirements page says insurance it maintains can cover up to $1,000,000 while you are on an active delivery and does not replace personal insurance. Skip’s help centre requires you to submit your own vehicle insurance. If the insurer will not cover food delivery, stop.

### Step 3: Gather documents and apply to one app first

Have the licence, insurance, registration, proof you can work in Canada, and banking details the app asks for. Apply to the app with the most restaurants in the area you will actually drive. A second app is useful after you know your costs. Background checks take days. Do not promise yourself income next weekend until you are approved.

### Step 4: Buy only the gear the first app requires

You need a thermal bag that stands up on its own, a phone mount, and a way to keep the phone charged. Add a flashlight or headlamp if you will deliver after dark to houses with no numbers. Buy the second bag after you have seen real orders.

### Step 5: Separate the money

Put payouts in the account you chose with the gear. Keep fuel receipts and a note of business kilometres. A notes app is enough for week one. You will want a proper log before tax time.

### Step 6: Learn your zone on a short shift

Start with a two-hour window you already know: the dinner rush near a cluster of restaurants, not a random drive across the city. Decline offers that send you far from that cluster unless the pay clearly covers the return trip with an empty car. Park legally. A ticket can erase the shift.

### Step 7: Handle the food with care

Keep hot food upright, cold food cold, and drinks separate. Text from the app when you are stuck, not from a personal number. If a building needs a buzzer code, ask before you have circled the block twice. For alcohol, follow the in-app ID rules and do not leave the order with someone who cannot show valid identification.

### Step 8: Add a second app only after the numbers work

If the first week of net pay, per hour online, is acceptable for your life, add a second network so a dead hour on one app does not end the shift. Running three apps at once often means accepted orders you cannot reach on time. Two is plenty while you learn.

### Step 9: Set a stop rule

Decide in advance the latest you will drive, the weather you will not drive in, and the offer distance you will refuse. Fatigue and black ice cost more than a slow Thursday. Write the rule down.

## What is easy to miss on a food delivery shift in Canada?

Food delivery shifts in Canada lose unpaid time on stacked orders, apartment codes, and winter driving. Multi-order offers can make the second customer late. If the second pickup is in the wrong direction, decline the stack until you know which restaurants actually have food ready.

Standing in a lobby is unpaid. Take the photo the app wants and go. Winter is a busy season and an expensive one. Budget winter tires if you do not have them, and confirm the winter-tire rule where you drive. A summer fuel average will mislead you in January.

Restaurants remember couriers who keep the bag closed and skip the argument. A calm repeat presence at two or three busy kitchens beats chasing a heat map across the city.

## What mistakes do new food delivery couriers make in Canada?

New food delivery couriers in Canada lose money by accepting every offer, by waiting to tell the insurer, and by treating a promotion as a wage. The app is allowed to offer a short-pay trip that leaves you far from home. You are allowed to decline it.

Business use of a personal car is a change in risk. Find out before you need the policy. Promotions have fine print, caps, and end dates. If the shift only works because of a bonus, food delivery does not work yet on the base offer.

Buying three apps’ worth of branded gear, a roof sign, and a new mount before the first approval email spends money food delivery has not earned. A phone in your hand is a distracted-driving problem. Look up the penalty where you drive.

## Which tax and insurance rules apply to food delivery in Canada?

Food delivery income in Canada is generally self-employment income reported on [Form T2125, Statement of Business or Professional Activities](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html), and self-employed people pay the whole Canada Pension Plan contribution on the net, as described under [Contributions to the Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html). GST/HST for food delivery follows the [$30,000 small-supplier test](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html), not the [taxi and ride-sharing](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-specific-situations/taxi-ride-sharing-drivers.html) rule, and Nova Scotia’s HST is 14% on the CRA [charge and collect the GST/HST](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate.html) table. In British Columbia, [Uber delivery driver insurance in Canada](https://www.uber.com/ca/en/deliver/insurance/) has described many basic ICBC policies as allowing up to six days a month of commercial use, and that line is Uber’s description.

This is general information, not tax, legal, or insurance advice. Check your own facts with the CRA, with Revenu Québec if you live in Quebec, and with a licensed professional if the situation is more than a simple side gig.

Keep the platform’s pay summaries and your receipts. An app may send a T4A. Report the income if a slip is late or missing. Where that payout goes on the return is in [how to report side hustle income to the CRA](/side-hustles/report-side-hustle-income-cra-canada/). You cannot claim the full cost of a car you also use for personal trips. Keep a kilometre log if you want to claim vehicle expenses. How that log becomes a business percentage is in [vehicle expenses for delivery and rideshare](/side-hustles/vehicle-expenses-delivery-rideshare-canada/). The [startup-cost guide](/side-hustles/food-delivery-startup-costs-canada/) covers capital cost allowance.

The small-supplier test is worldwide taxable supplies, including associates, of $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Under that test you generally do not have to register. Go over $30,000 in one calendar quarter and the CRA’s when-to-register page says you register and start charging on the supply that put you over. Go over across four quarters, but not inside one quarter, and you stop being a small supplier at the end of the following month.

That small-supplier test is the one that applies to ordinary food delivery. Self-employed drivers who carry passengers for a platform follow the first-dollar rule on the CRA taxi and ride-sharing page. If you also drive people, read [rideshare driving](/side-hustles/rideshare-driving-side-hustle-canada/) and [GST/HST and commercial ride-sharing services (GI-196)](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/publications/gi-196/gst-hst-commercial-ride-sharing-services.html). GI-196 says a small supplier’s mandatory registration can apply only to the ride-sharing fares until combined taxable supplies go over $30,000, and then GST/HST applies to the other taxable work as well.

The rate you would charge, once registered, depends on the province. Confirm the rate, including Quebec, on the charge-and-collect table and with the [GST/HST calculator (and rates)](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate/calculator.html). Auto insurance is a provincial matter: private in most provinces, and the Uber page’s ICBC note is about British Columbia. The age of majority Skip uses is not the same age everywhere in Canada. City bylaws can restrict where you wait outside a restaurant. The phone call to the insurer has to be about your province, your city, and your policy.
