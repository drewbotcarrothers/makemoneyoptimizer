Food delivery in Canada means picking up restaurant and grocery orders and bringing them to customers through apps like Uber Eats, DoorDash and SkipTheDishes, using a car, bike, e-bike or scooter. You choose when to log in and get paid per delivery plus tips. Two provinces now set a minimum: B.C. guarantees $21.89 an hour for engaged time as of June 1, 2026, plus a per-kilometre allowance, and Ontario guarantees the general minimum wage ($17.95 from October 1, 2026) for time on assignments. Time spent waiting for orders isn't covered in either province.

<QuickAnswer>

- **B.C. floor:** delivery and ride-hail workers get at least $21.89 an hour for engaged time (from accepting a job to completing it) as of June 1, 2026, plus a 35- to 45-cent per-kilometre vehicle allowance. Waiting time is unpaid.
- **Ontario floor:** since July 1, 2025, the Digital Platform Workers' Rights Act guarantees at least the general minimum wage for work assignments ($17.60 until September 30, 2026; $17.95 from October 1, 2026). Tips don't count toward the minimum and must be paid out in full.
- **Reality check:** in February 2024, Toronto Uber Eats driver Muhammad Musharaf Hossen told CBC News he made $3 in total across three deliveries needing 10 to 20 km of driving. Earnings vary widely by city, time and vehicle.
- **Startup:** a smartphone, an insulated bag, a phone mount and, for car couriers, insurance that covers delivery use.
- **Tax:** report on Form T2125, deduct the business share of vehicle costs with a mileage log, pay CPP, and register for GST/HST after $30,000. The first-dollar GST rule applies to rideshare, not food delivery.

</QuickAnswer>

## Who food delivery suits

Food delivery is one of the easiest side hustles to start, because the app supplies the customers. It suits you if:

- You want flexible hours with no boss or client to manage.
- You already own a reliable car, bike or e-bike, and you know your city well.
- You can work the busy windows: weekday lunches, dinners, weekends and bad-weather nights.
- You're comfortable with variable pay and tracking your own costs.

It's a weaker fit if you need predictable income, if your car is expensive to run, or if your city's apps are oversaturated with couriers. Newcomers often start here; see [side hustles for newcomers](/side-hustles/side-hustles-for-newcomers-canada/) for other options.

## What the law guarantees: B.C. and Ontario

### British Columbia

B.C.'s Employment Standards Regulation sets a minimum hourly wage for delivery services workers and ride-hail services workers that applies only to **engaged time**: the time between accepting a job on the app and completing it (or the job being cancelled). The baseline was $20.88; the rate in effect as of June 1, 2026 is $21.89. If your app earnings in a pay period fall below that rate multiplied by your engaged hours, the platform must pay the shortfall. CBC reported the rules also set a 35- to 45-cent minimum per-kilometre vehicle allowance.

In the same 2024 CBC story, Bains explained that the gig minimum was set 20% above B.C.'s general minimum wage at the time to make up for unpaid waiting time. Critics, including Metro Vancouver Uber driver Kuljeet Singh, said it was "not the legislation we asked for."

### Ontario

Ontario's Digital Platform Workers' Rights Act came into force on July 1, 2025. It gives delivery and ride workers the right to at least the general minimum wage for each work assignment or on a pay-period basis. The general minimum wage is $17.60 from October 1, 2025 to September 30, 2026 and $17.95 from October 1, 2026. Tips aren't counted toward the minimum, and platforms can't withhold or deduct your tips or earnings except where law or a court order requires it.

### Everywhere else

Other provinces didn't have equivalent gig minimums when this guide was updated. Your pay is whatever the app offers plus tips, so tracking your real hourly rate matters even more. See [how to work out your real hourly rate on gig apps](/side-hustles/gig-app-real-hourly-rate-canada/).

## Real courier experiences

- **Muhammad Musharaf Hossen (Toronto).** During a February 2024 strike, Hossen told CBC News that as an Uber Eats driver he made "just $3 in total across three deliveries requiring 10 to 20 kilometres of driving," on top of insurance, gas and car loan payments. He said he was striking so other newcomers in gig work would get better pay. Uber said the "vast majority of drivers are satisfied."
- **Kuljeet Singh (Metro Vancouver).** An Uber driver quoted by CBC in August 2024 when B.C.'s minimum pay rules took effect, Singh welcomed the move but said "this is not the legislation we asked for," because waiting time stays unpaid.
- **The Ridefair Toronto report.** The same CBC story covered a report by Ridefair Toronto and the Rideshare Drivers Association of Ontario that found some ride-hail drivers earned $6.37 to $10.60 an hour after operating costs, based on 96 weekly earnings statements. Uber called the methodology "shoddy" and said its own median figure was far higher.

The pattern: gross pay per hour can look reasonable, but waiting time and vehicle costs decide what you actually keep.

Check each app's vehicle requirements for your city before you buy anything. Uber lists requirements by city, and Skip and DoorDash publish theirs in their Canadian help centres.

## How to judge an offer in seconds

Apps show the pay and distance before you accept. Set your own minimums and stick to them.

1. **Work out your cost per kilometre.** Add up a month of fuel, insurance, maintenance and depreciation, then divide by kilometres driven. Bikes and e-bikes are far cheaper per kilometre than cars.
2. **Set a pay-per-kilometre floor.** Your floor should clearly beat your running cost for the whole trip, including the empty drive back to a busy area.
3. **Set a pay-per-minute floor.** Divide the offer by the estimated minutes, then add a few minutes for restaurant waits and apartment drop-offs.
4. **Think about where the drop-off leaves you.** An order that ends in a busy restaurant area is worth more than one that strands you in a quiet suburb.
5. **Watch your acceptance habits.** Some apps reward high acceptance rates with perks, but accepting money-losing offers to keep a rate up rarely pays.

These floors are your own business decisions; there's no right number for every city or vehicle.

## Peak times, weather and seasons

Demand isn't spread evenly. Most couriers earn the most per hour by working fewer, better-timed shifts:

| Window | Why it's busy | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Weekday lunch (about 11 a.m.–2 p.m.) | Office and campus orders | Short downtown trips; good for bikes |
| Dinner (about 5 p.m.–9 p.m.) | The biggest daily peak | Best overall window in most cities |
| Weekend late morning | Brunch and grocery orders | Larger orders, often better tips |
| Bad weather | More people order in | Fewer couriers may be on the road; drive carefully |
| Big game nights and holidays | Spikes in pizza and wings orders | Check local sports schedules |

These patterns are common but not guaranteed; use your own shift logs to find the windows that pay best where you live.

## Running more than one app

Many couriers keep two apps open and take the better offer. If you do:

- Read each app's terms; don't accept two orders you can't deliver on time.
- Track earnings by app so you can see which one actually pays more after costs.
- Keep separate bags or compartments for hot and cold orders.
- Remember that each app reports your earnings separately; your T2125 should include all of them.

## The math on a shift

**Illustrative example (B.C.).** You work a three-hour dinner shift by car. Assume two of those hours are engaged time, you drive 40 km while engaged, earn $55 in app pay and $20 in tips, and your car costs you $0.30 per km to run (all assumptions; track your own).

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| App pay (assumed) | $55.00 |
| B.C. minimum check: 2 h × $21.89 + 40 km × $0.35 | $57.78 (so the platform tops up $2.78) |
| Pay after top-up | $57.78 |
| Tips (assumed) | $20.00 |
| Vehicle cost: 55 km total driving × $0.30 (assumed, including driving between orders) | –$16.50 |
| **Net for 3 hours** | **$61.28, about $20.43/hour** |

**Same shift in Ontario (from October 1, 2026).** The minimum check is 2 hours × $17.95 = $35.90, below the assumed $55 app pay, so no top-up. Net would be $55 + $20 – $16.50 = $58.50, about $19.50 an hour. Without the tips, it drops to about $12.83 an hour.

**In a province with no gig minimum,** a slow night with $30 in app pay and $10 in tips would net $23.50 for three hours, under $8 an hour. That's why tracking every shift matters. See [when to quit a gig app](/side-hustles/when-to-quit-a-gig-app-canada/).

## Your launch plan: the first 30 days

### Week 1: sign up and get ready

- Apply to two apps; approval and background checks can take time.
- Call your insurer before your first car delivery and confirm you're covered for delivery use. Check what the app's own insurance covers.
- Buy an insulated bag and phone mount; set up a mileage tracking app.
- Open a separate account for payouts.

### Week 2: first shifts

- Work two or three peak windows: weekday dinners and weekend lunches.
- Accept offers based on pay per kilometre and per minute, not just the total.
- Log start and end odometer readings, hours online and hours engaged.

### Week 3: optimize

- Find your best zones: restaurant clusters with short drop-off distances.
- Try running two apps at once if allowed, and compare offers.
- Note which restaurants make you wait; avoid them at peak times.

### Week 4: review the numbers

- Calculate your real hourly rate after vehicle costs for each app and time slot.
- Drop the worst-paying windows and apps.
- Set aside tax money from every payout.

### Days 31 to 90

| Weeks | Focus | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| 5–6 | Work only your two best time windows | Higher net per hour |
| 7–8 | Test bad-weather and late-night shifts | Find premium periods |
| 9–10 | Compare car vs bike/e-bike costs for downtown work | Lower costs |
| 11–12 | Decide whether to keep, scale back or switch apps | A clear real hourly rate |

## Daily routine and checklists

**Before each shift**

- [ ] Phone charged; power bank packed
- [ ] Bag clean and dry
- [ ] Odometer photo or mileage app started
- [ ] Weather gear in the car or bag
- [ ] Zone and time window chosen

**Offer checklist (decide in seconds)**

- [ ] Total pay ÷ total kilometres (pickup + drop-off)
- [ ] Estimated minutes, including restaurant wait
- [ ] Drop-off area: will you get another order nearby?
- [ ] Apartment towers and parking: extra time?

**After each shift**

- [ ] Log hours online, engaged time, kilometres, pay and tips
- [ ] Move a share of earnings into a tax savings account
- [ ] Note which restaurants and zones paid best

### Winter delivery checklist

- [ ] Winter tires, and check your province's seasonal tire rules
- [ ] Ice scraper, brush, washer fluid rated for cold, and a small shovel
- [ ] Warm, waterproof boots with good grip for icy steps and driveways
- [ ] Touchscreen gloves and a spare pair
- [ ] Phone kept warm; cold drains batteries quickly
- [ ] Extra time built into every estimate
- [ ] For e-bikes: store and charge batteries indoors

**Customer message templates**

> Hi! I've picked up your order from [restaurant] and I'm on my way. ETA about [x] minutes.

> I'm at your building. Is there a buzzer code or a side entrance I should use?

> Your order is at your door as requested. Enjoy your meal!

Short, friendly messages reduce missed handoffs and can help with ratings and tips.

## Insurance and safety

Personal auto insurance may not cover delivery use. The apps provide some insurance during deliveries, and Uber describes its coverage on its Canadian delivery insurance page, but coverage varies by province and by stage of the trip. Call your insurer and tell them you deliver food before your first shift.

Safety basics: never leave your car running unattended, follow traffic laws, use lights and reflective gear on a bike, and decline offers that feel unsafe.

## Taxes for delivery couriers

- **Income tax:** delivery pay and tips are self-employment income. Report them on Form T2125. See [how to report side hustle income](/side-hustles/report-side-hustle-income-cra-canada/).
- **Vehicle expenses:** deduct the business-use share of fuel, insurance, maintenance, lease or capital cost allowance, using a mileage log. Details in [vehicle expenses for delivery and rideshare](/side-hustles/vehicle-expenses-delivery-rideshare-canada/).
- **CPP:** 11.9% of net earnings between $3,500 and $74,600 in 2026 (QPP in Quebec).
- **GST/HST:** food delivery follows the normal $30,000 small-supplier test. CRA's first-dollar registration rule applies to taxi and commercial ride-sharing, not food delivery. If you also drive rideshare, you must register from your first ride.
- **Platform reporting:** apps report sellers' earnings to CRA under the digital platform reporting rules; keep your own records that match.

## Provincial notes

- **B.C.:** $21.89/hour engaged-time minimum (June 1, 2026) plus a per-kilometre allowance; platforms must top up shortfalls.
- **Ontario:** general minimum wage for assignments under the DPWRA ($17.95 from October 1, 2026); tips paid in full; written pay information required.
- **Quebec:** QPP replaces CPP; check Quebec's rules for your vehicle type.
- **Prairies and Atlantic Canada:** no gig-specific minimum at the time of writing, so compare apps carefully in your own city.
- **Winter everywhere:** snow and cold slow deliveries but can raise demand and tips. Winter tires and cold-weather gear are part of the job.

## What to watch out for

- **Unpaid waiting time.** Neither province's minimum covers time waiting for orders.
- **Vehicle costs.** Fuel, insurance and depreciation can quietly erase your margin on short, low-paying trips.
- **Insurance gaps.** Delivering without proper coverage can leave you personally liable after a crash.
- **Deactivation.** Apps can suspend accounts for low ratings or policy issues; keep records of disputes.
- **Oversaturation.** In some cities, too many couriers chase too few orders at off-peak times.
- **Safety.** Traffic, weather and late-night deliveries carry real risks.
