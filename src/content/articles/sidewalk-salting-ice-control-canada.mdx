## What does a salting and ice control side hustle involve in Canada?

You visit each client's property after a snowfall, before or during freezing rain, or on a freeze-thaw morning, and spread ice melt (or sand or grit) on the surfaces people walk on. Most residential jobs are a walkway, front steps, a driveway apron and sometimes a back path to the bins. Small commercial jobs (a corner store, a dental office, a small apartment building) add entrances and parking spaces.

There are two ways to sell it:

- **Per visit:** you charge each time you apply. Simple, and fair when winters are mild.
- **Seasonal:** a flat price for the season with a stated trigger (for example, "after any snowfall over X cm or any freezing rain warning"). Predictable for both sides, but you carry the weather risk.

Many snow-removal clients assume salt is included. Say clearly in your price sheet and contract whether it is.

## What are the liability rules for salting and ice control in Canada?

Snow and ice injuries lead to claims, and contractors can be named. In Ontario, the [Occupiers' Liability Act](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90o02) (section 6.1, added by [Bill 118](https://www.ola.org/en/legislative-business/bills/parliament-42/session-1/bill-118) in 2020) says no action for personal injury caused by snow or ice can be brought against an occupier or "an independent contractor employed by the occupier to remove snow or ice" unless written notice, with the date, time and location, is served within 60 days. A contractor who gets a notice must send a copy to the occupier who hired them. A judge can waive a missed notice for a reasonable excuse, and the rule doesn't apply if the person died.

What this means for a side hustle:

- **You are in the chain of liability** if you are hired to remove snow or ice. The 60-day notice rule names you specifically in Ontario.
- **Your records are your defence.** A dated log of when you applied, what, and where is the evidence you were there.
- **Your contract should say what you do and when.** "Apply ice melt to the front walkway and steps within X hours of a snowfall ending" is clearer than "keep it safe."
- **Insurance matters more than for plain shovelling.** Ask your insurer specifically about snow and ice operations; many general policies treat them differently. Our [side hustle insurance guide](/side-hustles/side-hustle-insurance-canada/) and [Apollo vs Zensurance](/side-hustles/apollo-vs-zensurance-side-hustle-insurance-canada/) cover how to ask.

Other provinces have their own occupiers' liability laws and limitation periods; confirm the rule where you work.

## How much salt should you use, and does the environment matter?

Use the least that does the job, and follow the product label. Environment and Climate Change Canada's assessment found that road salts, in sufficient concentrations, pose a risk to plants, animals and the aquatic environment, which is why it publishes a Code of Practice for road authorities ([ECCC](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/pollutants/road-salts/overview.html)). That code is aimed at municipalities, not side hustlers, but the same logic applies to a driveway: over-salting costs you money, harms lawns and gardens, and doesn't add safety.

The [Smart About Salt Council](https://smartaboutsalt.com/training/) runs "Essentials of Salt Management" training for snow and ice contractors, online in English and French, covering 14 modules including equipment calibration, snow-clearing technique and managing risk. Its [certification](https://smartaboutsalt.com/certification/) for contracting companies requires the course (completed within the past five years), an application with a self-assessment, and an annual report, with random program verifications; the council lists insurance premium discounts and eligibility for participating client contracts among the benefits. It is optional for a small residential route, but it is a useful credential when you pitch property managers.

Practical habits that use less product:

- **Clear first, then salt.** Ice melt on top of snow mostly makes slush.
- **Spread evenly.** A calibrated spreader beats a scoop thrown by hand.
- **Pre-treat before freezing rain** when the forecast is clear, if the product label supports it.
- **Use sand or grit** where you need traction in deep cold or near gardens.
- **Sweep leftover granules** off dry pavement for reuse.

## How do you price salting and ice control in Canada?

There is no published Canadian rate table for residential salting, so price it from your costs and your time, then check what local snow-removal companies list on their own websites. A per-visit price has three parts:

```text
Per-visit price = materials + drive share + your time + margin
  materials   = kg used × (bag price ÷ bag kg)
  drive share = (km per storm round × $/km) ÷ stops per round
  your time   = minutes on site ÷ 60 × your target hourly rate
```

**Illustrative example (assumed inputs):** 2 kg of the $46.98, 50 lb (about 22.7 kg) bag = about $4.14 of material; a 30 km storm round at $0.25/km shared across 15 stops = $0.50; 10 minutes on site at a $60/hour target = $10. Cost-plus-time: about $14.64, so a $20 per-visit price leaves margin for wasted trips and extra steps.

For seasonal contracts, multiply your per-visit price by the number of salting events you expect, using last winter's count from your own log, then add a margin for a bad winter or cap the number of visits included. The [snow removal pricing guide](/side-hustles/snow-removal-pricing-canada/) shows the same method for clearing.

### Service clause you can adapt

```text
Ice control: After each snowfall of [X] cm or more, or when freezing rain is
forecast, [Business] will apply ice melt to the front walkway, front steps and
[other areas] within [X] hours of the snowfall ending. Client will report icy
spots between visits by text to [number]. [Business] is not responsible for
areas not listed, or for ice that forms after a completed visit.
```

Have a lawyer review contract wording if you take on commercial sites.

### Visit log template

```text
Date | Time arrived | Time left | Address | Conditions (snow cm / freezing rain / refreeze)
Product | Amount (kg or scoops) | Areas treated | Photo taken (Y/N) | Initials
```

Take a timestamped photo of each finished walkway. It costs nothing and is the best record you can have.

## What mistakes do new salting side hustlers make in Canada?

- **Throwing salt on uncleared snow.** It wastes product and leaves slush that refreezes.
- **Not logging visits.** In a slip-and-fall claim, your log and photos are the evidence.
- **Promising "ice-free" walkways.** Promise a service at stated triggers, not an outcome.
- **Skipping the insurance call.** Snow and ice work is a specific question for your insurer.
- **Storing bags outside.** Wet salt clumps; a bin pays for itself.
- **Pricing salt as "included" by default.** It costs real money per visit.

## How is salting income taxed in Canada?

Salting income is self-employment income: report it on Form [T2125](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html), deduct ice melt, spreaders, insurance and the business share of vehicle costs, and pay CPP on net profit. Once your total taxable revenue passes $30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters, you must register for GST/HST ([CRA](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html)); see [GST/HST registration for side hustles](/side-hustles/gst-hst-registration-side-hustle-canada/). Keep receipts for every bag; [side hustle expense deductions](/side-hustles/side-hustle-expense-deductions-canada/) lists what counts.
