## How much does food delivery cost to start in Canada?

Food delivery in Canada costs about $75.34 for a lean gear kit, $133.23 for a standard kit, or $198.74 for a winter-ready kit, all with 13% Ontario HST, before any insurance change. Use your province's rate. The kits assume you already have a reliable car, a smartphone and a licence, and they don't include a car payment.

## Who should use a food delivery startup budget in Canada?

A food delivery budget fits people whose car starts in January, who can work evenings and weekends, and who will call the insurer before the first order. The lean kit fits a two-week test. The standard kit fits someone in a zone that sends pizza or stacked orders. The winter-ready kit fits regular shifts from November to March.

It's a poor fit when the car is near the end of its life, when one parking ticket would wreck the month, or when the dollars have to be the same every week. Quiet afternoons are unpaid.

Grocery batches, parcels and passengers are different shapes. ## How do ongoing costs and break-even work for food delivery in Canada?

Ongoing food delivery costs in Canada are fuel, wear, parking, the phone plan and any insurance change, and those repeating costs matter more than the bag.

**Fuel or electricity.** Use litres per 100 km, or kWh per 100 km, times today's price, times the kilometres you drove, including the drive home. This page doesn't publish a national gas price.

**Wear.** Oil, tires, brakes and the value the car loses are real even when no invoice arrives this week. Set aside a few cents per kilometre after each shift. That set-aside is a habit, not your tax deduction. Self-employed couriers claim actual [motor vehicle expenses](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/sole-proprietorships-partnerships/business-expenses/motor-vehicle-expenses.html) on the business-use share, with a log, not an employee per-kilometre allowance.

**Cash-out fees.** $1.49 per Uber Instant Pay or $1.99 per Skip Fast Cash adds up if you cash out every night. The weekly deposit is free.

**Parking, tickets and tolls.** $0 until they're not. One downtown ticket can erase a night.

**Phone.** Usually $0 extra. Check your plan before you assume you need a data add-on.

**Insurance.** A monthly endorsement or a higher premium is a fixed cost. If your insurer confirms the current policy covers delivery, write that confirmation down. If the quote is painful, the gear budget was never the decision.

### Gear payback at the pay floors

**Illustrative. Each figure is the kit cost divided by the provincial floor for engaged time, rounded up. Engaged time excludes waiting between orders, so real hours online are longer, and fuel and wear aren't subtracted. These are floors, not typical earnings. Verify the arithmetic.**

| Kit | Cost to cover | Engaged hours at Ontario's $17.95 | Engaged hours at B.C.'s $21.89 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lean | $75.34 | 5 | 4 |
| Standard | $133.23 | 8 | 7 |
| Winter-ready | $198.74 | 12 | 10 |

For example, $133.23 ÷ $17.95 = 7.42, so 8 engaged hours.

### Step 1: Add the one-time gear

Bags, mount, charger and any winter items.

### Step 2: Add the monthly fixed costs

Extra insurance, extra data and anything else you pay whether or not you work.

### Step 3: List the variable cost of one shift

Fuel, the wear set-aside, parking, tolls and any cash-out fee.

### Step 4: Subtract variable cost from the payout

Contribution of a shift equals the payout you received, including tips, minus the variable cost of that shift.

### Step 5: Divide startup and fixed costs by the contribution

Shifts to earn back the gear equal one-time startup divided by contribution per shift. Count hours from the moment you go online, including restaurant waits.

**Illustrative example.** This is an illustration with round numbers, not a forecast, an average or a promise. Replace every figure with your log.

Suppose you buy the standard kit for $133.23. The insurer confirms no extra premium, so monthly insurance in this sketch is $0. One evening the app shows $95 including tips. You drove 55 km. You measured 8 L/100 km and paid an assumed $1.55/L, so fuel is 55 × 0.08 × 1.55 = $6.82. You set aside $0.12/km for wear: $6.60. Parking was $0, and you took the free weekly deposit.

Variable cost = $13.42. Contribution = $95 − $13.42 = $81.58. You were online 4 hours, so this sketch is about $20.40 per online hour.

Gear payback = $133.23 ÷ $81.58 = 1.63, so two evenings like this one. The winter-ready kit, $198.74, takes three.

If the insurer wants an assumed $90 a month, that's about 1.1 more of these evenings every month before the bags are paid back, and a quiet month still owes the premium. A night with more highway and a parking bill is a different contribution. Don't average this example into your rent.

## Where can food delivery startup spending be lower in Canada?

- **Buy one bag you trust,** not three thin ones.
- **Use the phone plan you already have.** Add data only if you run out.
- **Skip the pizza bag and the second bag** until a week of real offers shows you're missing those orders.
- **Take the free weekly deposit** instead of paying $1.49 or $1.99 per cash-out.
- **Skip the roof sign, branded clothing or a car** bought for a seven-day test.

## Where shouldn't you cut food delivery spending in Canada?

Don't skip the insurer, a mount that stays put, or winter tires where the road requires them. Home or personal auto policies often exclude paid delivery, and DoorDash's Canadian coverage only steps in after your own policy has denied a claim. A claim denied after a crash costs more than any endorsement.

For the insurance, logbook and tax mistakes couriers make most, see the [food delivery mistakes guide](/side-hustles/food-delivery-side-hustle-canada/#what-to-watch-out-for).

A phone in your hand is a distracted-driving problem. Look up the penalty where you drive. Hot food needs a real insulated bag, not a grocery tote. Write the kilometres down in week one. This is not insurance advice. Get the quote while you can still walk away.

## Which tax rules apply to a food delivery budget in Canada?

Food delivery income in Canada is generally self-employment income reported on [Form T2125](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html), and self-employed people pay the whole Canada Pension Plan contribution, both the employee and employer portions, as described under [Contributions to the Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html). Bags, cables, and fuel are ordinary expenses, while a car is usually [capital cost allowance](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/sole-proprietorships-partnerships/report-business-income-expenses/claiming-capital-cost-allowance.html) on the business-use portion. GST/HST for food delivery uses the [$30,000 small-supplier test](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html), not the [taxi and ride-sharing](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-specific-situations/taxi-ride-sharing-drivers.html) rule, and Nova Scotia’s HST is 14% on the CRA [charge and collect the GST/HST](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate.html) table.

This is not tax, legal, or insurance advice. Confirm your facts with the CRA, with Revenu Québec if you live in Quebec, and with a licensed professional if the situation is more than a simple side gig.

Keep the platform’s pay summaries and your receipts. An app may send a T4A. You still report the income if a slip is late or missing.

**Vehicle log.** If you claim the car, keep a log for the year: date, destination, kilometres, and the business reason. Business use is business kilometres divided by all kilometres. Apply that percentage to eligible expenses such as fuel, insurance, and maintenance. Do not claim the family car as 100 percent business because you did food delivery on Friday.

**CCA.** Capital cost allowance recovers the cost of a car, and of other gear that lasts for years, over time on the business-use portion, on the T2125, in the class the CRA assigns. Do not deduct a vehicle in full in the month you start. Rates change. Look them up for the year you file.

**GST/HST.** Ordinary food delivery uses the small-supplier test: worldwide taxable supplies, including associates, of $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Under that, you generally do not have to register. Timing is on the CRA’s when-to-register page. If you also drive people, read [rideshare startup costs](/side-hustles/rideshare-startup-costs-canada/), the CRA taxi and ride-sharing page, and [GST/HST and commercial ride-sharing services (GI-196)](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/publications/gi-196/gst-hst-commercial-ride-sharing-services.html). GI-196 says a small supplier’s mandatory registration can apply only to the ride-sharing fares until combined taxable supplies go over $30,000, and then GST/HST applies to the other taxable work as well.

This guide does not explain input tax credits. If you register, confirm those rules with the CRA or a tax professional. If you are not registered, you do not charge the tax and you do not claim the credit.

Once registered, charge the rate for the province. Confirm it, including Quebec, on the charge-and-collect table and with the [GST/HST calculator (and rates)](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate/calculator.html), and set the tax aside. Search your city for a business licence rather than assuming couriers are exempt everywhere. This guide does not state a fee from another city.
