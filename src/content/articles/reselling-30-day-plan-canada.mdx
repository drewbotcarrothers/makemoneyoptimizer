<QuickAnswer>

- **Week 1:** list items you already own on one or two platforms, with honest photos and measurements.
- **Week 2:** learn each platform's fees and shipping rules, and set up a simple record of cost and sale price.
- **Week 3:** source a small batch only after you know what sells, with a maximum buy price per item.
- **Week 4:** ship, relist, and review margins after fees. No timeline guarantees sales or profit.

</QuickAnswer>

A 30-day reselling launch plan for Canada starts with items you already own in week 1, learns fees and shipping in week 2, tests a small sourced batch in week 3, and reviews margins in week 4. Starting with your own closet means you learn listing, pricing, and shipping before spending on inventory. It suits patient, organized people who can photograph flaws honestly and track every dollar.

Use it with the series: the [reselling side hustle guide](/side-hustles/reselling-side-hustle-canada/) explains platforms, [reselling startup costs](/side-hustles/reselling-startup-costs-canada/) sets the budget and fees, [how to make your first reselling sale](/side-hustles/reselling-first-sale-canada/) covers the first listing in depth, and [reselling pricing](/side-hustles/reselling-pricing-canada/) helps you price after fees.

## What happens in week 1 of a reselling launch plan in Canada?

Week 1 is listing what you own: pick platforms, photograph honestly, and get listings live.

### Days 1 and 2: Pick one or two platforms

Choose platforms that fit what you have: local pickup for bulky items, clothing apps for apparel. The [Depop vs Poshmark vs Vinted vs Facebook Marketplace](/side-hustles/depop-vs-poshmark-vs-vinted-vs-facebook-marketplace-canada/) comparison helps.

### Days 3 to 5: Photograph and measure

Shoot in daylight on a plain background, include close-ups of any flaw, and measure clothing flat. The Competition Bureau says the Competition Act prohibits [claims that are false or misleading](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/deceptive-marketing-practices/types-deceptive-marketing-practices/false-or-misleading-representations) in a material respect, so describe condition accurately.

### Days 6 and 7: List 10 or more items

Write clear titles with brand, item, size, and condition. Price using sold listings as a guide, and see the [pricing guide](/side-hustles/reselling-pricing-canada/) for fee math.

## What happens in week 2 of a reselling launch plan in Canada?

Week 2 is learning the costs: fees, shipping rules, and a record of every item.

### Days 8 and 9: Learn each platform's fees

Read the fee page for each platform you use. The [startup costs guide](/side-hustles/reselling-startup-costs-canada/) summarizes eBay.ca and Poshmark Canada fees as published, but confirm the live pages.

### Days 10 and 11: Set up shipping

Buy a small scale, a few mailers, and tape. [Poshmark Canada](https://poshmark.ca/posh_guide/how_to_get_overweight_shipping_label) says its label covers up to 2.5 kg without extra fees, with heavier parcels needing an upgrade deducted from earnings. For your own labels, Canada Post says its [Solutions for Small Business](https://www.canadapost-postescanada.ca/cpc/en/small-business.page) membership is free and its Snap Ship tool compares services and discounted rates.

### Days 12 to 14: Start a record

Create a spreadsheet with item, date bought, cost, platform, sale price, fees, shipping, and net. Closet items have a cost too; note what you originally paid if you know it.

## What happens in week 3 of a reselling launch plan in Canada?

Week 3 is a small, disciplined sourcing test, only after week 1 and 2 show what sells.

### Days 15 to 17: Decide what to source

Look at what sold and what got views. Choose one or two categories you understand.

### Days 18 and 19: Source with a buy limit

Set a maximum price per item based on likely sale price minus fees and shipping, and a total budget for the batch. Thrift stores, garage sales, and local listings are common starting points.

### Days 20 and 21: List the batch

Photograph, measure, and list the batch the same way. Record each item's cost the day you buy it.

## What happens in week 4 of a reselling launch plan in Canada?

Week 4 is shipping, relisting, and review.

### Days 22 to 24: Ship promptly

Pack securely and ship within each platform's handling time. Weigh every parcel.

### Days 25 and 26: Relist and adjust prices

Refresh stale listings with better photos or adjusted prices. Drop items that will not sell into a donate pile rather than letting them fill a room.

### Days 27 to 30: Review margins and plan month two

Calculate net profit per item after fees and shipping. Keep categories that earn, cut the rest, and decide your sourcing budget for next month. Seasonal opportunities are in [Black Friday and Boxing Day reselling](/side-hustles/black-friday-boxing-day-reselling-canada/).

### Illustrative example

A teaching sketch with assumed numbers, not a forecast: you buy a jacket for $8 and sell it for $40. Suppose platform fees total $8 and you absorb $2 of packaging. Net is $40 − $8 − $8 − $2 = $22 before tax and your time. Real fees depend on the platform's live fee page.

## What reselling launch mistakes should you avoid in Canada?

Common launch mistakes are buying inventory before listing anything, ignoring fees and shipping in prices, hiding flaws, guessing parcel weights, and not recording costs. More are in [reselling mistakes](/side-hustles/reselling-mistakes-canada/).

## Which tax rules apply when launching reselling in Canada?

Reselling as a business is self-employment income on [Form T2125](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html), and the self-employed pay the whole [Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html) contribution on net income. GST/HST registration applies once worldwide taxable supplies pass [$30,000](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html) in a single quarter or over four consecutive quarters. Occasional sales of personal items differ from a business; keep records so you can tell which is which. This is not tax, legal, or insurance advice.
