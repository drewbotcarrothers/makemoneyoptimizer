## Who does a newsletter side hustle in Canada suit?

A newsletter in Canada suits people who already explain one subject clearly: a trade, a hobby with real steps, a local scene, or a tool you use at work and can describe without confidential material. Evenings and a quiet hour on the same weekday are enough. A newsletter is a poor fit if you need rent from the first send, if you will not write when nobody replies, or if the address list would come from a spreadsheet you did not build yourself.

Free and paid are different products. The free issue is the proof you can finish. The paid tier is a second product after several free issues exist. A paid button on an empty archive is a price with nothing behind it.

## What does Canada's anti-spam law require before a newsletter email?

Canada's anti-spam law, CASL, applies when a commercial electronic message is sent to an electronic address. The CRTC's [FAQ](https://crtc.gc.ca/eng/com500/faq500.htm) says the three requirements are consent, identification information, and an unsubscribe mechanism. [Section 6](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/section-6.html) of the Act prohibits sending the message unless the person consented and the message identifies the sender, gives contact information, and includes an unsubscribe. The sender must keep that contact information valid for at least 60 days after the send.

Express consent is opt-in. The CRTC says the person must take a positive action, such as checking a box, and that the sender has to be able to prove consent. A message that only asks for consent is itself a commercial electronic message, so it is not a lawful way to collect that consent. Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada's [consent page](https://www.ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canada-anti-spam-legislation/en/getting-consent-send-email) says implied consent exists only in limited cases, such as an existing business relationship inside the time limits in the Act, and that implied consent is generally time-limited. Buying a list is not one of those cases.

This is not legal advice. Read the FAQ and the Act before the first send, including the exemptions, which this paragraph does not list in full.

Newsletter earnings in Canada cannot be promised as a rate per subscriber, because ten readers who pay and ten readers who only open are different results. What you keep depends on the list below. This guide does not publish a national newsletter income.

## How do you start a newsletter in Canada?

Starting a newsletter in Canada means picking one subject, writing issues before you ask for money, and collecting addresses only from people who opt in. The steps below are that sequence.

### Step 1: Name the subject in one sentence

"Notes on opening a service business in one Canadian city" is a subject. "Money" is not. If you cannot say who the issue helps, you do not have a list to invite yet.

### Step 2: Write three issues offline

Finish them in a document. Each one should answer a question a reader could have asked. If the third issue is hard, the schedule is already too wide. Narrow the subject before you open a tool.

### Step 3: Read the CASL pages

Use the CRTC FAQ and the ISED consent page. Decide how you will store proof of opt-in. Do not export a personal address book and call it a launch.

### Step 4: Open one sending tool and leave payments off

Substack is one tool whose fee page is linked above. Other tools exist. Pick one. Confirm its live price. Do not turn on a paid tier in the same hour you create the account.

### Step 5: Put your name, a way to reach you, and an unsubscribe on the issue

The Act requires identification and an unsubscribe on a commercial message. Use your name. A brand with no person behind it makes the identification line harder. Keep the contact path working for at least 60 days after a send.

### Step 6: Invite people who already talk to you, one at a time

A colleague, a group chat you belong to, a note under something you already published. The invite should point to a signup form. Do not paste their address into the tool yourself unless they asked you to.

### Step 7: Send the first free issue on the day you named

One send. Then stop and read what was confusing. Fix the next issue. Do not send a second email the same day to "boost" the open.

### Step 8: After four free issues, decide whether a paid tier has a reason

A paid tier needs something the free issue does not: a worked example, a template, a deeper note. If you cannot name that extra, stay free. Read the fee page again before you set a price.

### Step 9: Record the payout, including a month that is zero

A quiet month is still a record. When money arrives, note the gross, the platform cut, and the processor cut from the dashboard, not from this sketch.

## What should the first week of a newsletter in Canada include?

The first week of a newsletter in Canada should produce three drafted issues, a consent plan, and one free send or a clear reason you are not ready to send. A paid subscriber is not the goal of the week.

- **Day 1:** Write the one-sentence subject. List five questions that subject can answer.
- **Day 2:** Draft issue one. Cut anything you cannot source.
- **Day 3:** Draft issues two and three. Read the CRTC FAQ.
- **Day 4:** Open one tool. Add your name and check that an unsubscribe exists. Leave payments off.
- **Day 5:** Ask five people, in a conversation, whether the subject is useful. Point them at a form if they want it.
- **Day 6:** Send issue one only to people who opted in. If the list is empty, the form is the work, not a purchased list.
- **Day 7:** Note what took longest. That hour is the schedule, not a slogan about consistency.

## What newsletter mistakes are common in Canada?

Common newsletter mistakes in Canada are emailing people who did not opt in, turning on payments before an archive exists, and treating a US card-processing example as a Canadian fee. CASL puts the proof of consent on the sender. A checkout link is not that proof.

Copying another writer's issue, or filling a send with unsourced income claims, creates a trust problem and can create a copyright problem. Write what you can check. Link the page the number came from.

A custom domain and a microphone are optional on Substack's cost page and in the gear table. They are not the product. Four honest issues teach you the hours. A paid button on a blank page teaches you nothing about whether anyone wanted the writing.

## Which tax rules apply to a newsletter side hustle in Canada?

Newsletter income in Canada, including a paid subscription, is self-employment income when you are carrying on that activity, reported on [Form T2125, Statement of Business or Professional Activities](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html), and self-employed people pay the whole [Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html) contribution on the net. GST/HST on your own taxable supplies follows the [$30,000 small-supplier test](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html), not the [taxi and ride-sharing](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-specific-situations/taxi-ride-sharing-drivers.html) rule. Nova Scotia's HST is 14% as of 1 April 2025 on the CRA [charge and collect the GST/HST](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate.html) table, and Quebec charges GST and QST.

This is not tax, legal, or insurance advice. Fee figures on platform pages should be rechecked when those pages change.

Keep the platform reports and the opt-in record. Report the newsletter on Form T2125 with the T1. The small-supplier test is worldwide taxable supplies, yours and your associates', of $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Add other side-hustle revenue to the same pot. Under that test, registration is usually optional. Over it, you register on the timetable on the CRA's when-to-register page. A newsletter is not ride-sharing, so the first-dollar rule on the taxi page does not apply unless you also carry passengers for a platform.

Once registered, charge the rate for the province where the supply is made. Some provinces use HST. Others add PST on top of GST. Confirm Quebec and every other province on the charge-and-collect table and with the [GST/HST calculator (and rates)](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate/calculator.html), and set the tax aside. Substack's cost page says some writers add sales tax or VAT on top of their price. A platform toggle is not a legal opinion. Check it against the CRA table.

Digital products and platforms that sell to people in Canada add complexity. The CRA's [digital-economy overview](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/digital-economy.html) describes rules, in effect since 1 July 2021, for certain non-resident suppliers and distribution platform operators. A checkout tax line is not your whole registration answer.

Search your city for a business licence and use that city's fee if there is one. This guide does not state a licence fee from another city.
