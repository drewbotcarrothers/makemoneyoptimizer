Mystery shopping in Canada is paid market research: you visit a store, restaurant, bank branch, or service as a normal customer, follow a checklist, and file a detailed written report for a mystery shopping company. It costs almost nothing to start, pays modest per-assignment fees (sometimes with a reimbursed purchase), and suits detail-oriented people who write clearly and want occasional pocket money rather than steady income. It's also one of the most common covers for job scams in Canada, so the scam section below matters as much as the how-to.

## Who does mystery shopping in Canada suit?

Mystery shopping in Canada suits people who notice details, follow instructions exactly, and write clear, objective reports soon after a visit. MSPA Americas' [shopper FAQ](https://www.mspa-americas.org/shopper-faq/) describes the job as market research rather than shopping, and says strong writing skills and the ability to follow directions without deviation are critical. It's a poor fit if you want predictable income: assignments depend on what clients need in your area, and the FAQ notes there may be no projects near you at first.

## How do you spot a mystery shopping scam in Canada?

You spot a mystery shopping scam in Canada by the money flow: a real company pays you after you complete a report, while a scam sends you money first and asks you to move some of it on. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre's [job scam page](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/job-emploi-eng.htm) describes the mystery shopper version this way:

- You get a notice that you've been "selected" as a mystery shopper, quality control officer, or similar, often with a cheque and instructions.
- You're told to deposit the cheque and transfer a large part of it to another account.
- You may be asked to buy gift cards and send the numbers "to confirm they work."
- You're asked to fill in a customer service survey at each step, which the CAFC says is a prop to make the job seem real.
- The cheque later turns out to be fake, and you owe the bank the money.

The CAFC lists general warning signs too: a job offer you didn't apply for, a web-based email address instead of a company domain, upfront deposits or "upgrade fees," a balance you can't withdraw, and requests to receive or forward money through your bank account. Its advice is to verify the company through its official website and phone number, never pay fees or deposits for a job, and report fraud to local police and the [Report Cybercrime and Fraud](https://reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca/) site.

MSPA Americas also recommends signing up only with companies from its current member directory, to avoid spoofed scam websites, and says it neither evaluates nor recognises paid "how to become a mystery shopper" programs sold by outside for-profit companies.

| Legitimate assignment | Scam |
| --- | --- |
| You apply on the company's own website | You get an offer you never applied for |
| You complete the visit, then get paid | You get a cheque or e-transfer before doing anything |
| Any purchase is reimbursed after the report | You're told to buy gift cards and send the numbers |
| No fee to join | "Registration," "certification," or "upgrade" fees |

## How do you start mystery shopping in Canada?

Starting mystery shopping in Canada means choosing reputable companies, building a profile, and accepting a few nearby assignments to learn the reporting style.

### Step 1: Decide if you want pocket money or a hobby

Mystery shopping pays per visit, and MSPA notes payment can take from a week to 60 days. Treat it as occasional extra money.

### Step 2: Find companies from a trusted list

Start from the MSPA Americas member directory, or its [Shoppers' Choice list](https://www.mspa-americas.org/), which names companies its shopper members voted as favourites. Go to each company's site directly instead of clicking links in unsolicited emails.

### Step 3: Create profiles carefully

Fill in demographics and location accurately; companies match assignments to those details. Never send ID or banking details by text or social media.

### Step 4: Read the brief twice

Each assignment has timing, purchase, and observation requirements. Missing one detail can mean the report is rejected and unpaid.

### Step 5: Write the report right after the visit

Objective, specific notes beat opinions. Note names, times, and what happened, not what you think should have happened.

### Step 6: Track every assignment and payment

Log dates, fees, reimbursements, mileage, and when you were paid. That log is your tax record.

## What mistakes are common in mystery shopping in Canada?

Common mystery shopping mistakes in Canada are falling for cheque scams, accepting far-away assignments, and filing late or vague reports.

- **Depositing a stranger's cheque.** No real assignment asks you to send money back.
- **Paying to join.** Legitimate companies don't charge shoppers.
- **Ignoring travel time.** A small fee across town can pay less than nothing.
- **Late reports.** Many companies reject reports after the deadline.
- **Mixing reimbursements with income.** Track them separately so your records are clean.

## Which tax rules apply to mystery shopping in Canada?

Mystery shopping income in Canada is generally self-employment income, because shoppers are usually independent contractors. The CRA's [employment status page](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/canada-pension-plan-cpp-employment-insurance-ei-rulings/employee-self-employed/determine-employment-status.html) explains how that's decided. Report fees on [Form T2125](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html), along with expenses like mileage for assignments. GST/HST registration only becomes mandatory once taxable sales pass the [$30,000 small-supplier threshold](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html).
