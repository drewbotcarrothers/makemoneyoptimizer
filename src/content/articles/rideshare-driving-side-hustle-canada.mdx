## Who is rideshare driving a fit for in Canada?

Rideshare driving in Canada fits people who like passengers in small doses, already drive calmly, and own a car that meets the local age and door rules. Rideshare driving is a poor fit when the vehicle is older than the city’s limit, when strangers or a messy car are a problem, or when the household has one car that the family needs every evening.

Rideshare driving is also a poor fit if winter night driving is already past a personal limit. A busy map does not change the road. Passengers are a different job from parcels. Grocery batches and [package courier work](/side-hustles/package-courier-side-hustle-canada/) are the closer choice when conversation, or a passenger who has been drinking, is the part to avoid.

A predictable paycheque is a different job. Rideshare driving pays when rides exist.

## Which vehicle and driver rules apply to rideshare driving in Canada?

Rideshare driving in Canada starts from Uber’s national vehicle floor, and cities can be stricter: Toronto, Mississauga, Oakville, and Brampton require a vehicle 7 years or newer, while elsewhere in the GTA the floor is 10 years or newer. The in-app checklist is the list that counts. Uber is the rideshare app described on the Canada pages linked here; other apps may operate in some cities, so read the signup flow before you buy mats.

[Uber vehicle requirements in Canada](https://www.uber.com/ca/en/drive/requirements/vehicle-requirements/) set this national floor:

- 4-door vehicle
- 10 years or newer, and for UberX the model year must be 10 years or newer
- Good condition with no cosmetic damage
- Not salvaged or rebuilt
- No commercial and/or Uber branding (the required decal is a separate rule on the same page)
- Registered in your name (Uber accepts official and temporary registration)
- Pass a vehicle inspection by a licensed mechanic

[Uber driver requirements by city](https://www.uber.com/ca/en/drive/requirements/regulatory/) is the GTA example of a stricter local list. Each Ontario city on that page has its own block. For the Greater Toronto Area the page says:

- Vehicle models must be 7 years old or newer in the City of Toronto, the City of Mississauga, the Town of Oakville, and the City of Brampton
- Vehicle models must be 10 years old or newer elsewhere in the GTA
- Drivers must be 21 or older with a full G licence or equivalent (G1 and G2 are not accepted)
- A safety standards certificate is required; new drivers upload it within 36 days of passing the inspection, and existing drivers complete a vehicle inspection annually
- Keep a driver’s licence, vehicle registration, proof of insurance, and safety standards certificate in the car; the GTA block also lists a PTC driver’s licence
- The name must be visible on the Ontario insurance pink slip; a secondary driver includes the full policy document
- You are not required to own the vehicle; the national vehicle page still says the vehicle needs to be registered in your name
- All-weather tires must be installed from December 1 until April 30 the following year in Toronto and Mississauga

That page also says Uber submits a Private Transportation Company (PTC) driver’s licence application to the City of Toronto after documents and the background check, that the licence can take up to 20 business days, and that the PTC licence allows requests in Toronto, Mississauga, Oakville, and Brampton. Other cities on the same page use their own submission rules.

[Uber driver requirements in Canada](https://www.uber.com/ca/en/drive/requirements/) says drivers meet the minimum age in the city, are legally allowed to drive, show proof of residency, and provide registration and insurance when using their own car. Screening reviews the driving record and criminal history. Documents to keep in the car are the driver’s licence, vehicle registration, proof of insurance, and a safety standards certificate, plus any required Uber sticker. The in-app checklist is the list that counts.

## What does rideshare driving pay in Canada?

Rideshare pay depends on time of day, events, airport rules, waiting time and how many drivers are online. The public data is sobering, and two provinces now set minimums.

| Benchmark | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Toronto median earnings after expenses, per engaged hour (2024) | $15.35 | City of Toronto study by Young, Farber and Rahman |
| Toronto median earnings after expenses, per in-app hour (2024) | $5.97 | Same study |
| B.C. minimum for engaged time | $21.89/hour (June 1, 2026), plus a 35–45¢/km vehicle allowance | B.C. Employment Standards; CBC |
| Ontario minimum per work assignment | General minimum wage: $17.95/hour from October 1, 2026 | Ontario Digital Platform Workers' Rights Act |

**What "engaged" means.** In the Toronto study and in the B.C. and Ontario rules, engaged time runs from accepting a trip to completing it. Time spent waiting for the next request is not covered. That's why the Toronto in-app figure ($5.97) is so much lower than the engaged figure ($15.35). The study counted fuel, insurance, depreciation, maintenance, repairs, financing and taxes as expenses.

**B.C.** Since 2024, B.C.'s Employment Standards Regulation sets a minimum wage for ride-hail and delivery workers, which applies to engaged time only. The baseline was $20.88; the rate in effect as of June 1, 2026 is $21.89. If your earnings in a pay period fall below that rate times your engaged hours, the platform tops you up. CBC reported the rules also set a 35- to 45-cent minimum per-kilometre vehicle allowance and WorkSafeBC coverage.

**Ontario.** The Digital Platform Workers' Rights Act came into force on July 1, 2025. It guarantees at least the general minimum wage for time on work assignments ($17.60 until September 30, 2026; $17.95 from October 1, 2026). Tips don't count toward the minimum and must be passed on in full.

**Elsewhere,** no provincial minimum applies to app-based drivers, so your log is the only real measure.

For per-trip math, see [rideshare earnings in Canada](/side-hustles/rideshare-pricing-canada/).

## Real Canadian rideshare drivers (publicly reported)

These are drivers quoted in news coverage. Their experiences aren't typical or promised.

**Kuljeet Singh (Vancouver).** CBC reported on March 18, 2026 that Singh, a ride-hail driver and director of the Ride Hailing Driver Association of B.C., was paying an extra $20 to $25 per fill-up. He fills up every three to four days while driving for Uber and Lyft, which he estimated at an extra $150 to $200 a month. **Lesson:** fuel is your biggest variable cost, and a price spike comes straight out of your pay.

**Earla Phillips (Ontario).** In the same CBC story, Phillips, vice-president of the Rideshare Drivers Association of Ontario, said she had been driving for companies like Uber and Lyft for nearly a decade. She described margins as already low before the fuel increase and said many drivers had turned to food banks. **Lesson:** long-time drivers organise because earnings after costs are thin, so run your numbers before you finance a car for this work.

**Toronto drivers on strike (2024).** CBC reported in February 2024 that ride-share and food delivery drivers in Toronto held a strike over pay and working conditions. A Ridefair report cited at the time estimated earnings of $6.37 to $10.60 an hour after expenses. **Lesson:** the screen total is not your wage. Subtract costs and include waiting time.

## Worked math: one B.C. shift and one Ontario shift

**Illustrative.** Fares, kilometres and fuel use are assumptions. The minimum rates come from the B.C. and Ontario sources above.

**B.C. shift.** Assume 4 hours online, 2.5 hours engaged, 80 engaged km, and a screen total of $70 before tips.
- B.C. minimum check: 2.5 h × $21.89 + 80 km × $0.35 (low end of the allowance range) = $54.73 + $28.00 = $82.73. Under these assumptions the platform tops up $12.73, for $82.73 before tips.
- Fuel: 110 km driven in total at an assumed 8 L/100 km and an assumed $1.80/L = $15.84.
- GST slice (5% GST in B.C., treating the total as tax-included): $82.73 × 5/105 = $3.94.
- Left after fuel and GST: $82.73 − $15.84 − $3.94 = $62.95, or about **$15.74 per online hour** before insurance, wear, CPP and income tax.

**Ontario shift.** Same hours, with 2.5 engaged hours and a $55 screen total.
- Ontario minimum check: 2.5 h × $17.95 = $44.88. The $55 screen total is above the minimum, so no top-up applies.
- Fuel: $15.84 (same assumptions).
- HST slice (13%, tax-included): $55 × 13/113 = $6.33.
- Left after fuel and HST: $55 − $15.84 − $6.33 = $32.83, or about **$8.21 per online hour** before insurance and wear.

The gap between the two shows how much waiting time and local rules matter. Replace every input with your own trip log.

## How do you start rideshare driving in Canada?

Start rideshare driving in Canada by confirming the city and the car, getting the insurance answer in writing, passing the inspection the city wants, and logging a short daytime shift before the hours get longer. Use the checklist for the province where the car is plated.

### 1. Confirm the city is open and the car qualifies

Enter the city in the driver app. Check model year, doors, and licence class against that city’s page. If the car is one year too old, stop. A failed inspection or a deactivation wastes the signup. If the car fails the rideshare age rule, open the delivery app’s own vehicle list before assuming the same car is accepted for meals. Insurance still applies. See [food delivery](/side-hustles/food-delivery-side-hustle-canada/).

### 2. Talk to the insurer, then read the platform policy

Tell the insurer you will carry paying passengers hailed through an app. Ask whether a rideshare endorsement exists, what it costs, and which minutes it covers: app off, app on and waiting, trip accepted, and passenger on board. Get the answer in writing.

[Uber driver insurance in Canada](https://www.uber.com/ca/en/drive/insurance/) says Uber maintains commercial auto insurance through Economical from the moment you are available to accept a ride until the trip is done. While you are online and waiting, personal coverage may apply depending on the province, and coverage may also be available through that Economical policy. Your personal policy must still be in force for personal use. You are responsible for telling the insurer. Uber Black and Uber SUV are different: those drivers keep their own commercial insurance, and Uber’s page says Uber does not maintain insurance for those products. Confirm the certificate for the product you will actually drive.

British Columbia ride-hailing is not an Ontario endorsement. Uber’s insurance page says a driver in British Columbia keeps a personal policy for personal use and for time on the app before a request is accepted, and that Uber maintains commercial coverage after a request is accepted. [ICBC ride-hailing insurance](https://www.icbc.com/insurance/commercial/ride-hailing) says basic insurance during ride-hailing use is arranged for an approved company, that optional ICBC products exist, and that Passenger Transportation Board requirements include a Class 1, Class 2, or Class 4 licence issued in B.C. This guide states no ICBC premium. Quebec licensing sits with the [SAAQ](https://saaq.gouv.qc.ca/en/); that site is general Quebec licensing, not a ride-hail fee. Saskatchewan and Manitoba use public auto insurers. Use the checklist for the province where the car is plated. A forum post from another province is not your policy.

### 3. Book the inspection the city wants

Many cities want a safety standards certificate or equivalent from a licensed mechanic, and they want it renewed on a schedule. In the GTA, [Uber driver requirements by city](https://www.uber.com/ca/en/drive/requirements/regulatory/) describes an annual inspection and a safety standards certificate uploaded within 36 days. Book the inspection before you promise yourself a start date. Fix bald tires and a cracked windshield first.

### 4. Pass the background check and upload clear documents

Licence, proof of insurance with your name on it, registration, profile photo, and residency documents. [Uber driver requirements in Canada](https://www.uber.com/ca/en/drive/requirements/) describes the profile photo and the residency documents. If you are a secondary driver on a household policy, Uber’s GTA notes say you may need the full policy pages, not only the pink slip. Other cities can ask for the same. Blurry photos delay approval.

### 5. Set the car up for passengers

Mount the phone. Put a charger in reach. Add rubber mats. Empty the trunk so a suitcase fits. Choose a cabin temperature and a default of low music. Decide the rule for food and drink before someone asks. A one-line house rule is easier than an argument at a red light.

### 6. Learn the pickup

Apartments, airports, and downtown hotels are where new drivers lose time. Pin the pickup, message once if you cannot stop legally, and leave the streetcar lane, bike lane, and bus stop clear. The passenger is watching the dot and rating the wait.

### 7. Drive a short first shift in daylight

Two or three hours, in weather you already handle, in a part of the city you know. Decline trips that strand you far away unless the return is worth it. Keep a log: hours online, hours with a passenger, kilometres, gross fares, tips, fuel.

### 8. Add winter judgment

Where winter is real, carry a scraper, washer fluid rated for the cold, a blanket, and a charged battery pack. Leave extra following distance. Uber’s GTA page requires all-weather tires in Toronto and Mississauga from December 1 until April 30. Confirm the winter-tire rule for the province where the car is plated. If the city is under a weather warning you would not drive your own family in, log off.

### 9. Decide with the log

One strong hour is not a business. If the net after vehicle costs is thin, try a different window once. If rideshare driving is still thin, stop. The car you save is worth more than a stubborn second month.

**Week 1: Qualify**

**Week 2: Set up**

- Install the mount, charger, mats and dash cam. Put the decal on the rear passenger window.

**Week 3: First shifts**
- Drive two or three short daytime shifts in areas you know well.
- Log hours online, engaged hours, kilometres, screen total and tips after every shift.
- Learn airport and event pickup rules from the in-app cards before joining any queue.

**Week 4: Measure**
- Calculate net per online hour: earnings minus fuel, minus a wear allowance, minus the GST/HST slice, divided by hours online.
- Compare day-of-week and time-of-day blocks, and keep the two or three best.
- Check that platform top-ups match your engaged time if you're in B.C. or Ontario.

- **Weeks 5–6:** Test one new block, such as early mornings for airport runs or weekend evenings, against your current best.
- **Weeks 7–8:** File or prepare your first GST/HST return and reconcile it with the platform's tax summary.
- **Weeks 9–10:** Get quotes on winter tires before the December rush if your city requires them.
- **Weeks 11–12:** Decide with the log: keep driving, cut back to the best blocks only, or switch to [food delivery](/side-hustles/food-delivery-side-hustle-canada/) or [package courier work](/side-hustles/package-courier-side-hustle-canada/).

## 18 ways to raise your net per hour

1. Drive your log's best two or three blocks instead of long open-ended shifts.
2. Log off during the dead hours your log shows.
3. Track engaged vs. online time so you can see your waiting cost.
4. Decline trips that take you somewhere you can't get a return trip from, when the app allows it.
5. Plan fuel stops at cheaper stations on your normal routes.
6. Keep tires properly inflated and the car serviced, which helps fuel use and safety.
7. Stage near busy areas where stopping is legal, not in loops.
8. Learn airport rules and staging lots, and only queue where allowed.
9. Know your city's event calendar: concerts, games, conventions.
10. Keep the cabin clean, since ratings affect your account standing.
11. Keep your acceptance and cancellation habits within the platform's rules so your account stays in good standing.
12. Turn on both Uber and Lyft where you're approved, and pause one when you accept a trip.
13. Check B.C. or Ontario top-ups against your own engaged-time log.
14. Claim every eligible expense with receipts: fuel, insurance share, maintenance, phone share.
15. Claim input tax credits on business-portion GST/HST once registered.
16. Log every business kilometre, not just the trips.
17. Avoid financing a newer car only for rideshare until your log proves the income.
18. Stop when you're tired. A crash or ticket wipes out weeks of earnings.

## What is easy to miss in rideshare driving in Canada?

The waiting period is where insurance arguments start for rideshare driving in Canada, because Uber’s insurance page says coverage while you are online and waiting depends on the province. Screenshot the explanation that matches the province where the car is plated, and keep it with the policy.

Airport and major-event pickups have local licences, staging lots, and fines. Read the in-app airport card before you join a queue you are not allowed to join.

A steady habit is boring on purpose: confirm the name, drive the speed limit, skip the personal questions, and end the trip only when the person and the bag are out. A forgotten phone is a support-ticket problem.

Track business kilometres from day one. A reconstructed log in April is how people lose the vehicle deduction. [Capital cost allowance](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/sole-proprietorships-partnerships/report-business-income-expenses/claiming-capital-cost-allowance.html) is how a lasting vehicle cost is claimed over time, on the business portion.

If you also deliver food on the same account, the insurance and the tax rules are not identical for the two activities. A rideshare endorsement and a food-delivery disclosure are separate conversations. Commercial ride-sharing has its own GST/HST rule, described below. Food delivery does not automatically follow that first-fare rule.

## What mistakes do new rideshare drivers make in Canada?

The expensive mistakes in rideshare driving in Canada are buying a newer car before the insurance quote, driving on a household policy that excludes passengers for hire in the province where the car is plated, and treating GST/HST as optional under $30,000. The car payment can outrun the fares. The gap between app-off and app-on is where claims get ugly.

Skipping the logbook is next. The CRA's [motor vehicle records page](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/sole-proprietorships-partnerships/business-expenses/motor-vehicle-expenses/motor-vehicle-records.html) says to log each business trip's date, destination, purpose and distance; after a full base year, a three-month sample can be used if business use stays within 10%. Not saving for tax is close behind: the CRA's [instalments page](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/payments/payments-cra/individual-payments/income-tax-instalments.html) says instalments may be required if net tax owing is more than $3,000 ($1,800 in Quebec) in 2026 and in 2025 or 2024.

Skipping winter tires because the app did not ask for a photo is the next mistake. Toronto and Mississauga have an all-weather tire window on Uber’s regulatory page. Other provinces set their own dates. Confirm the rule where the car is plated.

Chasing every busy pocket into a neighbourhood you cannot leave is the last one. Busy pockets shrink. The bridge back does not.

## How do insurance, provinces, and tax apply to rideshare driving in Canada?

Insurance for rideshare driving in Canada is provincial, and GST/HST on commercial ride-sharing starts with the first fare even when you are under the small-supplier threshold. This guide is general information, not tax, legal, or insurance advice. Use the checklist for the province where the car is plated.

Ontario drivers typically need their private insurer to know about ride-hailing, and many insurers sell a specific endorsement. Alberta is also a private market with its own products. British Columbia ride-hailing is not an Ontario endorsement: read [Uber driver insurance in Canada](https://www.uber.com/ca/en/drive/insurance/) and [ICBC ride-hailing insurance](https://www.icbc.com/insurance/commercial/ride-hailing), and do not invent a premium from this page. Quebec drivers still deal with Quebec licensing through the [SAAQ](https://saaq.gouv.qc.ca/en/) and with Revenu Québec on the tax side. Saskatchewan and Manitoba use public auto insurers. None of those provincial systems is a reason to copy another province’s forum post.

Self-employment tax still applies. Fares are business income. Keep summaries, receipts, and a kilometre log. Report the income on [Form T2125](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2125.html) with the T1. Self-employed drivers generally pay the whole [Canada Pension Plan](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/contributions.html) contribution on net self-employment income. A T4A from the platform does not replace your own records. [Capital cost allowance](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/sole-proprietorships-partnerships/report-business-income-expenses/claiming-capital-cost-allowance.html) spreads the business portion of a lasting vehicle over time. You cannot deduct a personal vehicle in full, or the whole purchase in year one.

The CRA says self-employed taxi operators and commercial ride-sharing drivers must register for GST/HST even if they are small suppliers, and must charge GST/HST on those fares. The registration steps, including Quebec QST, are in [when to register for GST/HST as a side hustler](/side-hustles/gst-hst-registration-side-hustle-canada/). The kilometre log is in [vehicle expenses for delivery and rideshare](/side-hustles/vehicle-expenses-delivery-rideshare-canada/). The effective registration date is the day you start supplying the rides. The sources are [GST/HST for taxi and ride-sharing drivers](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-specific-situations/taxi-ride-sharing-drivers.html) and [GI-196](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/publications/gi-196/gst-hst-commercial-ride-sharing-services.html). Commercial ride-sharing, in that info sheet, is passenger transportation arranged with an electronic platform. Registering gives you a business number; the online steps are in [how to get a CRA business number](/side-hustles/how-to-get-cra-business-number-canada/).

GI-196 also says that if you are otherwise a small supplier, the mandatory registration can apply only to the ride-sharing services. You do not automatically charge GST/HST on unrelated side work, and you do not claim input tax credits on that other work, unless you ask to register more broadly or your combined taxable supplies exceed $30,000. The CRA’s own example: $20,000 of ride-sharing plus $15,000 of other taxable supplies is $35,000, you are no longer a small supplier, and you charge tax on all of it.

The $30,000 small-supplier test still matters for other work. The CRA’s test is worldwide taxable supplies of $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Details are on the CRA’s [when to register](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html) page.

Charge the rate for the province where the supply is made. Participating HST provinces differ from GST-only provinces. Quebec adds QST on top of GST. Confirm the current QST rate before you add it. Nova Scotia’s HST has been 14% since 1 April 2025 ([Charge and collect the GST/HST](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate.html)). Use that table or the CRA [GST/HST calculator](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate/calculator.html). Remit what you collect. Spending the tax is how side hustles become tax debts.

Provincial rates to know: Ontario 13% HST, Nova Scotia 14% HST and Alberta 5% GST.
