After this guide you can decide if a snow route fits your body and your climate, price a driveway before the first storm, and have a written agreement ready so a low-snow winter does not wreck either you or your clients.

Snow removal is the side hustle Canadians talk about in October and regret improvising in January. The money, when it comes, comes at 5 a.m., in the dark, on ice, with a city plough burying the end of the driveway an hour after you finished. The people who do it well sell a defined service to neighbours they can reach before work. The people who get hurt sell “I’ll just bring a shovel” with no insurance and no limit on how often they come.

If you already cut grass for the same houses, you are halfway to a year-round route. Read [lawn care](/side-hustles/lawn-care-side-hustle-canada) for the summer half and [handyman work](/side-hustles/handyman-side-hustle-canada) for the gate and stair repairs you will see once the snow melts. If you do not want predawn physical work, this is not your winter plan. [House cleaning](/side-hustles/house-cleaning-side-hustle-canada) does not care whether the sky falls.

## Who it fits

**This can fit if** you can get up early, you are steady on your feet, you have a place to store salt and a shovel, and you live where snow is a repeating event rather than a rumour. It fits people with a vehicle that starts in the cold and a back that can shovel a trial route before they buy a machine. It fits lawn clients who already trust you.

**This is a poor fit if** you have a cardiac history or a back injury you are hoping will “be fine.” It is a poor fit in a mild coastal city where you might shovel twice all winter and you just financed a two-stage blower. It is a poor fit if you cannot leave a day job on storm mornings, if you have nowhere legal to store fuel, or if you want passive income. Most of the value is a clear walk before the commute, which means working while the household is still asleep. If that is unacceptable in October, it will not improve at minus 20.

## Where snow is a business, and where it is a favour

Canada does not have one winter.

- **Prairies, Ottawa, Montreal, Quebec City, and much of Atlantic Canada:** snow is a season. A route can be a real side income if you price it as a season. Storms stack. You need a plan for the week it never stops.
- **Southern Ontario, away from the snowbelts:** variable. Some winters are a handful of events. Some are relentless. Seasonal contracts need a trigger depth and a visit cap or you will either overcharge a mild year and feel guilty, or undercharge a harsh one and feel ruined. The snowbelts around the Great Lakes are a different climate from downtown Toronto. Price the street you are actually on.
- **British Columbia:** do not finance a blower on a mild coastal winter. The Fraser Valley, the interior, and the north still get real snow. A Surrey or Kelowna route is not a Kitsilano route.
- **The North:** fewer clients, harder cold, and machines that must start at temperatures southern manuals treat as a footnote.

Many cities require owners to clear sidewalks within a set number of hours. The clock is local. You are helping them meet that duty. Do not push snow into the road, onto a hydrant, a bus stop, or a neighbour’s cleared walk. Read the city’s winter page before you promise the sidewalk. In some places the city clears it and you must not interfere.

City ploughs create windrows, the ridge at the end of the driveway. Clients feel that ridge as your problem even when the plough came after you. Your agreement should say whether a return visit for the windrow is included, extra, or not your job.

## What you are selling

Sell one of these, not a vague “call me when it snows.”

**Per visit.** Simple. You come when they call or when accumulation hits a number you both use. Income dies in a mild winter. You are also easy to forget at 5 a.m. because they have not called yet.

**Seasonal contract.** A set price for the season, with a trigger depth (for example, you clear when settled snow reaches a stated depth), a daily time window, a list of surfaces, and a cap or a fair-use note so a once-in-a-decade winter does not mean unlimited free visits. This is the product that lets a client budget and lets you plan a route. It needs writing.

**Hybrid.** A seasonal price for a defined number of visits, then a per-visit rate after that. Easier to explain than unlimited service. Good in variable southern Ontario winters.

Write down, in plain language:

- The address and what is included: driveway, front walk, steps, city sidewalk, windrow
- Trigger depth and who measures it
- The time you will be done by, and what happens if a storm is still falling at that hour
- Start and end dates of the season
- Price, when it is due, and the extra-visit rate
- Salt or traction product: included, extra, or never, and whether they want a pet-safer product
- What you will not do: roofs, unless you are equipped and insured for it (most side hustlers should not do roofs), city streets, or the neighbour’s half “as a favour” with no price
- How either of you can cancel
- A line that this note is your working agreement, not a lawyer’s document. Roofs and ice dams stay off the menu. They injure people, and casual insurance will not treat them as a shovel job.

## Costs in CAD

Start with a shovel route. A blower is a tool you earn with a full morning, not a personality you finance in September. Ranges below are typical Canadian retail and used prices to verify locally. They are not quotes. The full lean-versus-blower budget is in [snow removal startup costs in CAD](/side-hustles/snow-removal-startup-costs-canada).

| Item | Typical CAD range | How to think about it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ergonomic shovel, and a second smaller one for steps | $40–$90 | Your first eight to fifteen walk-ups should be possible with this alone. |
| Ice melt, 10–20 kg | $15–$40 a bag | Pet-safer and concrete-safer blends usually cost more. Budget repeats. |
| Headlamp and spare batteries | $20–$40 | You will work in the dark. A phone flashlight is not the tool. |
| Grip boots or overshoes you trust on ice | $40–$140 | This is safety equipment. Cheap smooth soles are a claim waiting. |
| Used single-stage snow blower | Often $400–$1,200 if it runs | For paved, relatively flat driveways and modest snow. See it start cold if you can. |
| New single-stage blower | Often about $800–$1,500 | Buy new when used machines are tired. Confirm current shelf prices. |
| Two-stage blower for deeper snow and gravel | Often $1,500–$3,500 new | Heavier, better in the Prairies and in big dumps. Storage and maintenance go up. |
| Shear pins, fuel, oil, spark plug | $30–$80 to start | Pins break on purpose when the auger hits a newspaper. Carry spares. |
| Commercial general liability insurance | A quote | Slip-and-fall is the risk that ends casual operators. Get the quote before you take a seasonal cheque. |
| Truck or trailer | Only if the route demands it | A neighbourhood you can walk or reach in one already-owned vehicle does not need a trailer payment. |

## Earnings, including the winter that barely comes

No one can promise a seasonal total. A prairie route of modest driveways and a Vancouver route are not the same product. What you keep depends on:

- How many properties you can finish in the time window, on foot or with a blower, without rushing on ice
- Trigger depth and how many events actually hit it
- Whether windrow return visits are included
- Salt costs in an icy year
- Equipment repairs and fuel
- The morning you are sick and have no backup
- How often you “just do the sidewalk” for free and destroy the price

Sanity-check a quote with three sketches under your own trigger: a light winter, a typical one, and a heavy one. If the heavy year pays you less per hour than a hard shift at your day job, rewrite the cap or the price. Compare with a local invoice, not the highest number on social media. A seasonal deposit is working capital for salt, fuel, and repairs. It is not a blower down payment and a trip.

## Step-by-step

### 1. Decide the route shape in daylight

Walk the blocks you could clear before your other life starts. Time a pretend loop with an empty shovel. Note hills, gravel driveways, long townhouse walks, and hydrants. Gravel and a single-stage blower are a poor marriage. Stones go through windows.

### 2. Read the sidewalk rule and the dumping rule

Quote only what your city’s winter page allows. If the contract includes the sidewalk, it includes the sidewalk on the ugly days too.

### 3. Get an insurance quote with the real work described

Say residential driveways and walks, whether you use a blower, whether you apply ice melt, and that you do not clear roofs. Ask about slip-and-fall. Home insurance often excludes business activity. A seasonal cheque without liability coverage is a bet against someone’s broken wrist. This is not insurance advice. Ask a broker while they are still answering October calls.

### 4. Write the offer before the snow flies

One page: trigger depth, surfaces, time window, season dates, price, windrow rule, salt rule, extra-visit rate, payment dates. Use centimetres, not “a good dump.” Two centimetres and twelve centimetres are different jobs. A common small-route trigger is in the 2 to 5 centimetre range. Pick one and use it everywhere so your morning decision is fast.

### 5. Price three sample driveways on your street

Price a single-car driveway, a double, and the corner lot with steps. Add salt and the walk back. If you are far cheaper than a local operator, you missed something. A full route of underpriced driveways is how people quit in February.

### 6. Sign clients in the fall

October and early November are when responsible owners buy peace of mind. A polite door knock, a note, or your existing lawn clients are enough. Collect a deposit or the first instalment when they agree, and put it in the separate account. Confirm the trigger by text so there is a record.

### 7. Stage the gear before the first forecast

Shovel by the door, lamp charged, salt stored so it does not turn into a brick, blower tested on a dry day, shear pins in the glove box. The first storm is when everyone else is buying the shovel you already own.

### 8. Run the first real storm as a drill

Start at the far end so you finish near home, unless a hill says otherwise. Clear to the trigger you promised, not to perfection on house one and a rush on the last. Text when you are done if you said you would. Do not block the neighbour.

### 9. Hold a mid-season check

After three events, look at hours, salt used, and whether the windrow rule is fair. Adjust extras for new clients. Honour the written deal for current ones. If a property is unsafe — black ice you cannot manage, an aggressive dog, a driveway you cannot exit — cancel that one contract politely. A route you dread is how injuries happen.

## Your first week

Do this before the snow, in late September through November in much of the country.

- **Day 1:** Walk a route and note ten properties. Read the sidewalk and snow-dumping rules.
- **Day 2:** Insurance quote. If you cannot get coverage you understand, do not take seasonal money.
- **Day 3:** Write the one-page agreement. Pick the trigger depth and the done-by time.
- **Day 4:** Price three driveway types. Buy a shovel, a lamp, and one bag of ice melt. Test your boots on a slick surface.
- **Day 5:** Talk to five households, starting with people who already know you.
- **Day 6:** Follow up. Take one or two clients. Deposit any money in the separate account.
- **Day 7:** Stage the gear, order the route in your phone, and name a backup if you get sick.

## Tips that are easy to miss

Salt is not a substitute for shovelling. Clear first, then use a thin, even melt where ice remains. Piling salt on a surface you did not scrape wastes money and burns concrete and paws. Pet-safer products still are not candy. Tell clients if you use them, and follow the bag rate.

Mark edges in the fall. Stakes at the driveway corners and along garden beds save you from eating a ornamental rock with the auger. Clients who garden will thank you in May. Offer to place the stakes when you sign them.

Do the windrow return you wrote down, and do not add free ones all winter. Shear pins are supposed to break. Turn the machine off and pull the key before you clear a clog. Fingers do not grow back. Tell someone your route and when you should be home. And put a light-winter sentence in the October agreement: retainer, visit cap, or a small credit. Do not invent the refund in April.

## Common mistakes

Buying the two-stage blower before the clients. Storage and a loan payment do not care that the route is still a theory, and a mild year will not cover that payment.

Unlimited clears with no trigger depth. You will be out for flurries, or you will argue about flurries.

Pushing snow into the street, onto a hydrant, or onto the neighbour. Stack it on the client’s property, where the city allows.

No liability policy, plus more houses than you can finish before 7:30 a.m. The last house gets the worst job and the first cancellation. Grow next storm, not this one.

## Canada note

This is not tax, legal, or insurance advice.

Snow income is self-employment income, including a seasonal lump sum. A deposit in November is still income to track. Keep the agreements, the e-transfers, and the receipts for salt, fuel, and repairs. Report it on form T2125 with your T1. Net self-employment income generally means both shares of CPP. If you use a vehicle to haul equipment, keep a kilometre log for any vehicle claim. Do not claim the family car as 100 percent business because you drove to a driveway.

GST/HST follows the small-supplier test: worldwide taxable supplies, yours and your associates’, of $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Add lawn, cleaning, and handyman revenue to the same pot. The CRA explains the timing on its [when to register](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html) page. Snow removal is not ride-sharing, so the taxi first-dollar rule does not apply unless you also carry passengers for a platform.

Once registered, charge the provincial rate. Some provinces use HST. Others add PST on top of GST. Quebec charges GST and QST. Nova Scotia’s HST is 14% as of 1 April 2025. Use the CRA [rate table](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate/calculator.html) and set the tax aside.

Your contract does not erase negligence. Clear what you promised and carry liability insurance that matches the work. Search your city for a business licence. If you pay a helper, check that province’s workers’ compensation board before you call them a favour. Store fuel the way the can and your municipality describe, not on a balcony.
