## The quick answer

Ranges are typical Canadian retail, to check locally. They assume a reliable car with a clean trunk and a phone that runs the shopper app. They are not a quote and they do not include buying a car.

| How you start | One-time cash, about | What you actually have |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lean | $70–$160 | Two sturdy totes, one large insulated bag, and a charger. You already have data and a car, and you have asked the insurer about paid delivery. |
| Typical | $180–$400 | A second, smaller insulated bag for meat and ice cream, a phone mount, a trunk liner, and a folding cart if your first buildings have stairs. |
| Doing it properly | $350–$800, plus any insurance change | The typical kit, a fuel float for the first two weeks, shoe covers or a blanket you are willing to stain, and room for the insurance answer. A walk-up route without a cart is how backs become the hidden cost. |

Instacart’s Canadian shopper pages say you must be at least 18, allowed to work in Canada, able to lift up to 40 pounds (18 kg), and have a reliable car and a smartphone. Signup asks for a driver’s licence, social insurance number, and bank details for direct deposit, and the shopper contract requires consent to background checks. That is the public floor. The app may also ask for insurance proof. Zones open and close with store contracts. If no stores appear, do not buy the cart.

Other grocery apps come and go by city. Budget from the app that actually shows stores near you.

## Who this budget fits

**It fits** organized shoppers who can lift, text a stranger about yogurt, and work when other people shop: weekday evenings, Saturday mornings, the day before a holiday. The lean budget fits a hatchback that is already empty.

**It is a poor fit** if you cannot lift, if downtown meters will eat every batch, or if the only car is full of child seats. A 40-item order with stairs is not a tote problem. It is a body and a parking problem.

Restaurant bags are a different kit. So are pre-packed parcels. Compare [food delivery startup costs](/side-hustles/food-delivery-startup-costs-canada) and [package courier startup costs](/side-hustles/package-courier-startup-costs-canada). Passengers are a stricter tax and insurance problem: [rideshare startup costs](/side-hustles/rideshare-startup-costs-canada).

## Ongoing costs, and a simple break-even

**Fuel and wear.** Same method as any delivery job: your consumption, today’s price, the kilometres from home to store to door to home. Add a cents-per-kilometre planning set-aside for tires and oil. That set-aside is not automatically the tax deduction. A log of actual expenses is what a vehicle claim usually needs.

**Parking.** The line grocery shoppers forget. Some stores do not validate. Downtown meters and parkades are a cost centre. Write the amount on the batch, not “about a few dollars” at the end of the month.

**Bags and ice packs you replace.** A torn insulated bag is a small repeat purchase, often under $80 when it finally dies. Do not replace it weekly.

**Platform take.** Grocery apps show a batch total before you accept, and they take a fee according to their current terms. Those terms change. Read them in the Canadian account you use. Do not paste a percentage from a video into the spreadsheet. Tips in Canadian cities are uneven: some customers tip in the app, some add cash, some tip nothing on a heavy order. Budget the amount on the screen. Treat a tip as extra when it arrives.

**Phone.** Usually the plan you have. A $5–$30 data add-on only if you hit the cap.

**Insurance.** A monthly figure once you have the quote. $0 only with a written “your current policy covers this.”

Unpaid time is a cost even when no receipt exists: out-of-stock substitutions, a gate code that does not work, a customer who wants the shop redone in the chat. Log hours from arrival at the store.

### How to calculate break-even

1. One-time startup (totes, bags, liner, charger, cart if you bought it).
2. Monthly fixed (insurance difference, data, a software fee only if you pay one).
3. Variable cost of one batch (fuel, wear set-aside, parking).

Contribution = batch pay you actually received, including any tip that landed, minus variable cost.

Batches to earn back the gear = startup ÷ contribution.

Batches per month to cover fixed costs = monthly fixed ÷ contribution.

Divide by hours from store arrival to the last door if you want a planning rate.

### Illustrative example

**Arithmetic with pretend inputs. Not a typical batch and not income you should expect.**

Totes, a large insulated bag, and a blanket cost $110. No cart yet. Insurance quote in this sketch is $0 extra because the insurer confirmed coverage. You accept a batch that pays $42 including a modest tip. You drove 18 km. At 9 L/100 km and $1.50/L, fuel is 18 × 0.09 × 1.50 = $2.43. Wear at $0.10/km is $1.80. The store lot was free. The customer’s street had a meter: $3.50.

Variable cost = $7.73. Contribution = $42 − $7.73 = $34.27. The shop and the delivery took 1.5 hours including two substitutions. This sketch is about $23 per hour for that batch.

Gear payback = $110 ÷ $34.27, or about four batches like it.

Now change one fact. The same pay with a $12 parkade and 30 km of driving drops the contribution hard, and a third-floor water case that makes you skip the next hour has an opportunity cost the receipt will not show. If the insurer had wanted $70 a month, you would need about three of these $34 batches a month before the bags start to pay themselves back. Track the real batches for a week. Throw this example away.

## Where you can spend less

Use two good totes and one real insulated bag. Skip the matching set of six.

Borrow a folding cart for a week, or buy a basic one used, before you buy a stair climber. Renting a cart is rarely worth the errand. Owning a cheap one is.

Shop stores you already know. Unpaid time in a strange superstore costs more than a better bag. Cluster batches from the same store when you can. Dead kilometres are the leak. The app pays you, so a spreadsheet is enough for a test week.

## Where not to cut corners

Do not deliver frozen food in an open tote to save $40. The complaint costs the batch and the rating.

Do not skip the insurer because “it is only groceries.” The car is still being used for pay. This is not insurance advice. It is the line that keeps a claim from being a surprise.

Do not skip lifting limits. Instacart’s public Canadian standard is 18 kg. A back injury is not a line item you can shop around.

Do not turn your phone brightness down and guess at substitutions to save battery. Wrong meat and wrong produce are the quality failure in this job. Carry the charger.

## Canada note

This is not tax, legal, or insurance advice.

You are generally self-employed. Report the income, keep batch summaries and receipts, and expect that a year-end slip may arrive as a T4A. You still report income if the slip is missing. Net self-employment income is generally subject to both shares of CPP. The form self-employed people use with their T1 is T2125.

**Vehicle log and CCA.** Claim the car only for the business portion. The log is the date, the destination, the kilometres, and the reason. Business kilometres divided by total kilometres is the percentage you apply to fuel, insurance, and maintenance. Bags and liners you use up are ordinary expenses. A vehicle is capital. Capital cost allowance (CCA) spreads that cost over time on the business-use portion, in the class the CRA assigns. Do not deduct the purchase of a car in full because you started shopping apps. Look up the year’s rules or ask an accountant if the vehicle is the large claim.

**GST/HST.** Grocery shopping and delivery are not commercial ride-sharing. The first-dollar GST/HST rule is for taxi and passenger ride-sharing. Ordinary shopping services use the small-supplier test: worldwide taxable supplies of $30,000 or less in one calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters, including associates and your other hustles. Cross that line and registration stops being optional. Timing is on the CRA’s [when to register](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/when-register-charge.html) page. If you also carry passengers, those fares follow the stricter rule. Read the CRA’s [ride-sharing page](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-specific-situations/taxi-ride-sharing-drivers.html) before you mix the two.

**Input tax credits.** If you register, GST/HST paid on business purchases can often be recovered as an input tax credit to the extent the purchase is for commercial activity. Keep tax-showing receipts. If you are not registered, you do not charge the tax and you do not claim the credit. It stays inside the expense.

Rates depend on the province. Some use HST. Others add PST to GST. Quebec charges GST and QST. Nova Scotia’s HST is 14% as of 1 April 2025. Use the CRA [rate table](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/gst-hst-businesses/charge-collect-which-rate/calculator.html).

Tip culture, parking prices, and which stores are on the app are city facts. There is no national batch rate to copy. Search your city for a business licence rather than assuming couriers are exempt everywhere.
